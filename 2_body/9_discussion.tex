\Cref{sec:results} found the central claim to hold as measured, within its stated scope: carrying the derivatives instead of eliminating them keeps the kernel and lowers the Hamiltonian's norm from $\Theta(N^8)$ to $\Theta(N^2)$ on a second-order equation with constant coefficients, and with it the degree from about $N^4$ to about $N$. The price is a block register and a gap the derivative blocks erode, which block rescaling restores on one axis without ancilla but not on two. The measurements depart from the formulation chapters on singular problems (\Cref{def:regular}), on two axes and on a lift past its boundary, and at the ideal-encoding bound the standard form needs the lower degree on R3 and on the heat equation at small $n$. Exponents record how fast a degree grows, but they do not explain why carrying the derivatives as unknowns lowers the Hamiltonian's norm and endangers its gap at the same time, nor what a lower degree is worth once gates, amplification and the classical alternatives are counted, nor how far a study made entirely of classical computation can vouch for a quantum algorithm. This chapter asks \emph{what mechanism produces the descriptor's advantage and its limits, and what the measurements imply beyond the benchmark problems.} The answer it develops is that ground-state preparation of a differential equation costs what the conditioning of its residual costs, and that the formulation, rather than the circuit, decides that conditioning.

\subsection{Conditioning of the Two Forms}
\label{sec:disc-tradeoff}

The structured encoding holds the standard form's subnormalisation to within a constant of its norm, and the degree still grows as $N^{2k}$ (\Cref{tab:growth}), so the exponent must be from the residual itself. Consider factorisation $\G=\Bt^{-1}\Dt$ of \Cref{eq:G-banded}. In $\|\G\|_2$, there are two powers of $N$, one is $\|\Dt\|_2=2(N-1)$ and the other comes from the inverse, $\Bt^{-1}$ being a triangle of ones on each parity class. For an order $k$ equation, we compose $\G$ $k$ times. So, $\|R_{\mathrm{std}}\|_2=\Theta(N^{2k})$ for constant coefficients, and the Gram product doubles the exponent again to $\|\Hstd\|=\Theta(N^{4k})$. The descriptor keeps the banded factors, which avoids the inverse, and carries each derivative as its own unknown, which avoids the composition. Every recurrence row applies $\Dt$ and $\Bt$ once, uninverted. For fixed $\zeta$ and any order equation, no block exceeds a $\mathcal O(N)$ norm, and $\|\Hdesc\|=\Theta(N^2)$ (\Cref{sec:descriptor-spectrum}).

The descriptor measures a state's residual against $\sum_m\|\G^mp\|^2$ (\Cref{eq:descriptor-quotient}), the same quantities the standard form's norm cost incorporates. A state with large carried derivatives compared to its residual has a small Rayleigh quotient. The reformulation therefore relocates the conditioning of differentiation rather than removing it: from a norm that grows with $N$ on every problem, to a gap that falls only where the equation fails to control one of the derivatives it carries. \Cref{thm:flat-gap} proves that the control holds on a regular problem, and both ways in which the gap falls are failures of that control. Where the leading coefficient vanishes, a state concentrated near the zero has a top derivative that the equation does not penalise. On two axes, the equation constrains only a sum of top derivatives, so a state whose second derivatives along the two axes nearly cancel has a small residual (\Cref{sec:descriptor-gap}). The stiff case R2c shows how severe this can be even on a regular problem: its unscaled gap at $n=8$ is $\res[2,sci]{build/R2c/descriptor/n8/unscaled}{gap}$, against $\res[3]{build/R2c/descriptor/n8}{gap}$ rescaled (\Cref{sec:results-gap}). The mass form of a first-order equation removes the inverse without carrying any derivative, so its states are measured only by the field, which is consistent with its gap exceeding a chain's on R7 (\Cref{sec:systems-fields}). At $k\ge2$ no such option exists, and the chain form lowers the norm at the risk of the gap.

Classical numerical analysis met the same trade from two directions. First-order-system least squares carries the derivatives to avoid squaring a high-order operator's condition number. Its theory rests on norm equivalence: the requirement that the least-squares functional bound every carried unknown in its own norm \cite{cai1994fosls}. \Cref{thm:flat-gap} is a discrete, one-axis instance of coercivity, the lower half of that equivalence. The collapse on two axes is what its failure would look like. On two axes a high-frequency near-solution is penalised only by the datum rows of \Cref{sec:standard-constraints}, which weigh it at the order of its field norm, while the carried derivatives that dominate the denominator of \Cref{eq:descriptor-quotient} grow with $N$. That mismatch between the data on the boundary and the derivatives in the interior is the likely reason coercivity fails. We attempted weighting each datum row by a power of its tangential mode, so that the rows penalise high frequencies more. This raised the gap much more than the norm and lowered the cost by a modest factor at three and four qubits per axis, but the weighted gap still fell with every qubit, and a heavier weight performed worse (\Cref{app:descriptor-gap}). Thus, only reweighting the existing rows does not supply the missing coercivity. The ultraspherical method reaches the same banded factors and finds its systems well conditioned once diagonally preconditioned \cite{olver2013}, which is the role block rescaling takes here. The increment of this thesis is therefore not the idea of carrying derivatives through banded factors but its transfer to a block-encoded Hamiltonian, and the measurement of what it costs and where it fails.

The block register multiplies the state's dimension by $2^{n_f}$: four blocks at second order, and eight for Laplace's equation and the wave equation (\Cref{sec:app-variable-coeff}). A query applies more terms through that register, so on constant-coefficient problems it costs more two-qubit gates than the structured standard form's at the sizes built and verified, $\res{encode/R2a/descriptor/n2}{cx_count}$ against $\res{encode/R2a/standard/n2}{cx_count}$ on R2a at $n=2$, and the two converge to $\res{query/stateprep/R2a/descriptor/n8/T3/filter=minimax}{gates}$ against $\res{query/stateprep/R2a/standard/n8/T3/filter=minimax}{gates}$ at $n=8$ (\Cref{sec:results-scaling}). Block rescaling adds no ancilla, but it lengthens the Pauli expansion of the register's patterns by a fixed factor depending on the equation. Whether these costs are repaid is decided by the exponents, which depend on the class of problem. On a singular problem the rule's $\zeta\propto N$ raises the descriptor's norm to $\Theta(N^4)$ at second order, and on two axes or at a double zero no block weight restores the gap (\Cref{sec:descriptor-rescaling}).

\subsection{End-to-End Complexity}
\label{sec:disc-complexity}

Neither form is polylogarithmic in $N$. The degree of \Cref{eq:degree}, $d=\Theta\bigl(\sqrt{\alpha_H/\Delta}\,\log(1/\varepsilon)\bigr)$ with $\alpha_H\ge\|H\|$ for any block encoding, is the residual's effective condition number times $\log(1/\varepsilon)$. It has the form of the optimal linear-systems law, $\mathcal O(\kappa\log(1/\varepsilon))$ queries \cite{costa2022optimal}, which no algorithm improves to $\kappa^{1-\delta}$ (unless $\mathrm{BQP}=\mathrm{PSPACE}$ \cite{harrow2009hhl}). The ground-state route therefore meets the conditioning problem of linear systems by another path. Read this way, the standard form's $\Omega(N^{2k})$ for constant coefficients under every encoding, measured as $\tilde\Theta(N^{2k})$ with the best exact one (\Cref{eq:standard-wall}) is the conditioning of an order-$k$ least-squares discretisation, and the descriptor's $\tilde\Theta(N)$ on a regular problem (\Cref{eq:descriptor-degree}) is that of a first-order one. The reformulation is thus an analogue of the order reduction that linear-systems solvers for ordinary equations make before discretising (\Cref{sec:bg-de-linear}). For singular problems and on two axes the descriptor's degree is about $\tilde\Theta(N^2)$ or steeper. Without a source, or with a polynomial source of fixed degree, a query costs $\mathcal O(n^2)$ two-qubit gates in both forms (\Cref{prop:standard-cost,prop:descriptor-cost}), a general source adding $\mathcal O(N)$, and amplification multiplies the queries by $2k_{\mathrm{aa}}+1=\mathcal O(1/\gamma)$ (\Cref{eq:total-cost}). On a regular problem with constant coefficients a preparation therefore costs $\tilde\Theta(N\,\mathrm{poly}(n)/\gamma)$ gates in the descriptor form against $\tilde\Theta(N^{2k}\,\mathrm{poly}(n)/\gamma)$ in the standard form, whose $N^{2k}$ is proved only as a lower bound on the degree and is measured as an equality with the structured encoding. The overlap $\gamma$ is measured rather than bounded, and it differs between the forms, the descriptor's capped by the field block's weight, which the rescaling restores (\Cref{sec:results-gap}).

On R2a at $n=8$ (\Cref{tab:headline}) the exact encoding removes most of the encoder's cost of Wu et al.'s approximate one, what remains at the ideal bound is the Hamiltonian's, and the descriptor, whose encoding is also the tighter, is a different Hamiltonian that lowers it. Because the as-built comparison favours the descriptor, only the ideal bound compares the two Hamiltonians. A gate count per query, which is how \gls{pihm} prices differentiation, is accurate but incomplete, because it does not count the queries. Separating the encoder's cost from the Hamiltonian's is an audit that applies to any filter-based algorithm on a block-encoded operator.

\Cref{eq:standard-wall} holds for every block encoding of $R_{\mathrm{std}}$ under any polynomial filter of the walk, since by Chebyshev's extremal property none suppresses the excited spectrum at a lower degree than the minimax filter (\Cref{sec:bg-filtering}). Over all algorithms the evidence is weaker. Lin and Tong prove, by reduction from unstructured search, that their ground-state algorithm's dependence on the gap and the overlap is essentially optimal for an oracle that block-encodes $H$ \cite{lintong2020groundstate}. The walk here is built from an encoding of $R$ which supplies the square root in \Cref{eq:degree}. So their bound does not apply as it stands, and whether their hard instances arise as stacked residuals is not shown. We therefore claim the floor for polynomial filters of the walk, and for second-order examples with constant coefficients it follows that $\|\Hstd\|=\Theta(N^8)$ sets the number of queries under any block encoding, whatever circuit implements one query (\Cref{sec:standard-hamiltonian}). The descriptor lowers the floor by replacing $R$, which no statement about the encodings of a fixed $R$ excludes.

The same accounting applies to quantum linear-systems solvers of spectral discretisations. Such a solver needs $\mathcal O(\kappa\log(1/\varepsilon))$ queries, with $\kappa$ the subnormalisation of the matrix's block encoding over its smallest singular value \cite{costa2022optimal}, and the degree of \Cref{eq:degree} is about the same ratio for the residual, $\alpha_R/\sigma_1$, with $\sigma_1$ its smallest singular value above the kernel. Childs and Liu's spectral solver for ordinary equations already reduces the order, the step the chain takes, but discretises the first-order system by Chebyshev collocation, whose differentiation matrix is dense with a norm growing as $N^2$, and bounds the resulting condition number by a polynomial in the number of nodes \cite{childsliu2020spectral}. The mass form writes the same first-order equation through the banded factors $\Dt$ and $\Bt$, at a norm growing as $N$, and on a regular problem its gap is bounded below (\Cref{thm:flat-gap}). Applied to such a solver, the factorisation would be expected to lower the condition number by the same mechanism, and Olver and Townsend's bound, linear in $N$ for the square ultraspherical system without a preconditioner \cite{olver2013}, is the classical form of that expectation. It was not tested here. A linear-systems solver inverts a square system with the data on its right-hand side, whereas this thesis prepares the kernel of a homogeneous residual, so the transfer needs its own bound on the smallest singular value. If it holds, the factorisation serves quantum spectral solvers generally, and singular problems and several axes, where the descriptor's gap fails here, are where such a solver would have to check that bound first.

The \gls{gqsp} circuit realises the minimax edge filter and the \gls{qite} filter from the same walk at the same normalisation (\Cref{sec:bg-qsp,app:gqsp-phases}), so the polynomial can be isolated for comparison. At equal suppression imaginary time needs a constant factor more degree, and the factor does not drift with the resolution (\Cref{sec:results-faithful,app:filter-study}). The cases at equal measured infidelity and a loose target where it needs less arise because its exponential suppresses each excited state more the higher it lies, while the minimax filter holds all of them at the equiripple level. That dividend comes from the structure of the spectrum above the edge, which an a priori pipeline neither knows nor can certify, so the minimax filter, optimal for exactly the worst case that the edge alone determines (\Cref{prop:minimax-optimal}), is the one that answer-independent parameters can guarantee. The filter is thus an engineering constant worth a factor below two. On the problems measured, the growth of the degree with $N$ is set by the residual: through its norm in the standard form, and in the descriptor through its gap wherever the equation fails to control a carried derivative.

The prepared field's error is set by $\varepsilon$ from $n=4$ on R2a while the discretisation's own error keeps falling (\Cref{sec:results-faithful}), so resolution beyond what the solution needs only raises the degree. For an analytic solution a field accurate to $\delta$ needs $N$ growing as $\log(1/\delta)$, both degrees are then polylogarithmic in the precision, and the comparison of exponents in $N$ implies no speed-up. On one axis a degree linear in $N$ is no asymptotic advantage in any case: the ultraspherical method solves such an equation classically in $\mathcal O(N)$ operations once its coefficients are resolved \cite{olver2013}. An advantage could appear only on many axes, where a classical solver's cost grows at least as the number of unknowns, $N^D$ on $D$ axes. However that is exactly where the descriptor's gap falls. Its estimated degree grows as $N^{\res[2]{stateprep/R5b/descriptor/growth/T3/filter=minimax}{degree}}$ per axis on the heat equation and $N^{\res[2]{stateprep/R5a/descriptor/growth/T3/filter=minimax}{degree}}$ on Laplace's (\Cref{tab:growth}), faster than the $N^2$ unknowns. Whether a residual can keep its gap on several axes therefore decides whether this route can compete with classical spectral solvers at all. We leave that question open.

The split in the simple-zero row turns on where the coefficient vanishes. The standard form's norm is set by the top Chebyshev modes, whose derivatives are largest at the ends of the interval by Markov's inequality \cite{borweinerdelyi1995}. A coefficient that vanishes at both ends ($1-x^2$ on R3) suppresses those modes where they peak. There $\|\Hstd\|$ grows only as $N^4$. On R4a, $x-1$ vanishes at one end and the $N^8$ law holds (\Cref{sec:results-scaling}). The rescaled descriptor's norm grows as $N^4$ on both, so it gains on R4a but not on R3. This is an inference from two problems consistent with \Cref{sec:standard-hamiltonian} but not a theorem. It makes a testable prediction that no benchmark problem checks: an interior zero, which suppresses neither end, should follow the standard form's $N^{4k}$ law, so the descriptor formulation is expected to scale better there.

On the nonlinear rows the guidance follows $a/\nu$ rather than $\rho$, and the reason is physical. The ratio $\rho=a\|F_2\|/\nu$ is a worst case over all data of norm $a$. $\|F_2\|$ grows with the modes because the advection term couples into a mode with a strength that rises with its wavenumber (\Cref{sec:fluids}). Burgers' data $a\sin x$ occupy one mode and reach the others only through the nonlinearity, so at moderate strength the high modes (where $F_2$ is largest) carry too little of the solution to move the lift's error. This would explain why that error is the same on four modes as on sixteen (\Cref{sec:results-nonlinear}). The vortex pair puts several wavenumbers into its data. Its triads are active from the start, and the error does grow with the modes (\Cref{sec:results-ns}). Both readings are inferences from the measured errors. They agree with $\rho<1$ being sufficient and far from necessary, and explain why a worst-case ratio is a poor guide to a particular flow.

A trusted kernel certifies that the lifted linear problem has an isolated ground state, which is what the filter needs. However it says nothing about whether that ground state is the nonlinear solution. On Burgers at $a/\nu=10$ both forms prepare the lift to $\varepsilon$, and the decoded field is wrong by $\res[3]{stateprep/R9e-M4/descriptor/n5/T2/carleman=5/filter=minimax}{field_err_linf}$ against an amplitude of $10$. On Navier--Stokes at $a/\nu=30$ from $28$ modes the lift diverges with every kernel trusted. In neither case does anything in the residual's spectrum signal the failure (\Cref{sec:results-nonlinear,sec:results-ns}). Thus every Carleman preparation needs the lift's measured error per order beside it. The shorter window that restores convergence on $28$ modes is what the lift's time-dependent radius of convergence leads one to expect \cite{forets2017carleman}, and it is what \Cref{tab:applicability} recommends.

The measurements agree with the mechanism of \Cref{sec:prep-vs-proj}: the standard form wins at $n_t=3$ on Burgers, where the coupling dominates a coarse time axis, the descriptor gains from $n_t=4$ on, and the Navier--Stokes margin narrows as the modes grow at fixed $n_t$, in part through the looser encoding of the coupling's matchings. The same balance predicts a reversal on enough modes, not reached on up to $48$ (\Cref{sec:results-ns}). It assumes comparable gaps in the two forms and was not checked term by term, but its recommendation to raise $n_t$ with the truncation does not depend on that assumption. The reformulation's advantage therefore survives nonlinearity, carried by the one axis on which the lifted system still differentiates.

\subsection{Feasibility and Scope of the Classical Evidence}
\label{sec:disc-feasibility}

We assume a fault-tolerant machine for all cost analysis (\Cref{sec:bg-qc}). On R2a at $n=8$ the descriptor's preparation is estimated at $\res{stateprep/R2a/descriptor/n8/T3/filter=minimax}{n_qubits}$ logical qubits and $\res[2,sci]{stateprep/R2a/descriptor/n8/T3/filter=minimax}{gate_count}$ two-qubit gates, $\res[1]{stateprep/R2a/descriptor/n8/T3/filter=minimax}{preparations}$ amplified repetitions of a filter of degree $\res{stateprep/R2a/descriptor/n8/T3/filter=minimax}{degree}$, against $\res[2,sci]{stateprep/R2a/standard/n8/T3/filter=minimax}{gate_count}$ for the structured standard form and $\res[2,sci]{stateprep/R2a/standard/n8/T3/filter=minimax/encoder=published}{gate_count}$ with Wu et al.'s encoding (\Cref{tab:headline}). The qubit counts stay modest, with the widest circuits estimated, Navier--Stokes on twelve modes at $K=4$ and $n_t=5$, needing $\res{stateprep/R10c-M12/descriptor/n5/T3/carleman=4/filter=minimax}{n_qubits}$ qubits in the descriptor and $\res{stateprep/R10c-M12/standard/n5/T3/carleman=4/filter=minimax}{n_qubits}$ in the standard form. The obstacle is the gate count. All the costs of R2a above buy the solution of one second-order equation on $N=2^8$ coefficients, a problem of trivial classical difficulty. The reformulation divides the count by about the ratio of the two degrees, which grows as $N^{2k-1}$ on such a problem; that alone does not make the problem worth a quantum computer. The estimates count logical two-qubit gates after transpiling to $\{\mathrm{CX},U\}$ (\Cref{sec:pipeline-design}). They omit the non-Clifford gates from which an error-corrected machine synthesises the rotations $U$, whose count dominates its cost, along with the code's overhead and routing. They record no depth, which grows with the degree because the walks run in sequence. Jennings et al.\ cost a linear-systems route to linear \glspl{ode} in logical qubits and in queries to the block encoding of the system's matrix, with their constant factors \cite{jennings2023cost}. Their counts and ours meet at the query, whose gates are supplied here for both forms, but their examples are time evolutions of stable systems and are not compared with ours.

Depth rather than width relegates the method to error-corrected hardware at every size run. A filter of $\res{stateprep/R2a/descriptor/n8/T3/filter=minimax}{degree}$ sequential walks of $\res{query/stateprep/R2a/descriptor/n8/T3/filter=minimax}{gates}$ two-qubit gates each lies far beyond the depth that uncorrected processors execute reliably \cite{preskill2018nisq}, and even the smallest preparation, the decay $f'+2f=0$ (R1) at $n=2$ in the descriptor form, fits on $\res{stateprep/R1/descriptor/n2/T1/filter=minimax}{n_qubits}$ qubits but applies $\res{stateprep/R1/descriptor/n2/T1/filter=minimax}{degree}$ sequential queries of $\res{encode/R1/descriptor/n2}{cx_count}$ two-qubit gates each (circuit simulation). No preparation here is a candidate for a noisy device, and the variational methods that target such devices answer a different question.

Circuit simulation sets the ceiling for runs, since its statevector memory doubles with every qubit and its time grows with the degree (\Cref{app:t1-model}), and the resolutions at which the method's open question lives, several axes and many modes, lie beyond it (\Cref{sec:results-faithful}). This is a limit of the classical simulator rather than of the circuit. The exact emulation equals the circuit's post-selected output (\Cref{sec:pipeline-verification}), which licenses it to filter degrees of $2^{20}$, and we use a resource estimate beyond.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Every result is a classical computation, and nothing ran on hardware. The claims are cost laws of operators and filters, which classical computation tests exactly. It cannot test noise, and the method's robustness to it is unknown. Exact emulation stands in for the circuit beyond $\res{stateprep/R4d/descriptor/n3/T1/filter=minimax}{n_qubits}$ qubits by an argument that the agreement of the two confirms only where both run. The widest reflections, those of the two-axis descriptor problems and of the larger lifts, are built and counted but not verified whole, and Navier--Stokes is reached by emulation and estimate alone (\Cref{sec:results-ns}). The growth exponents are two-point slopes between stated resolutions, consistent with the laws rather than fits of them, and the two-axis slopes, between $n=4$ and $5$ per axis, are the least settled. Where $N$ must grow with the problem, as on a stiff equation, the dependence of the gap's constant on the stiffness was measured on R2c alone. Beyond $8{,}192$ physical columns the reference is iterative, and the error bound it reports is computed from the residual of the returned vector rather than proved, so it is reliable only if the iteration has converged and no singular value below $\tilde\sigma_1$ has been missed (\Cref{app:iterative-reference}).

The descriptor's scope ends where its gap does. The gap is proved flat only on regular problems, measured flat after rescaling on singular ones with a simple zero, and falls on two axes. A double zero needs the substitution of \Cref{sec:standard-constraints}, whose form presumes that the zero's order is known from the equation, and mixed derivatives have no block and are not supported (\Cref{sec:descriptor-constraints}). The full ultraspherical reduction, a banded residual on $N$ unknowns with no block register, would remove the inverse without carrying any derivative, but it was neither built nor compared (\Cref{sec:bg-ultraspherical}). Its differentiation operator is a single diagonal whose norm grows linearly in $N$ at every order, and for a leading coefficient that does not vanish, as many boundary conditions as the order and a uniquely solvable problem, Olver and Townsend bound its condition number after a diagonal preconditioner, in the 2-norm for Dirichlet conditions, so that without one it grows linearly with the truncation \cite{olver2013}. On a regular problem this suggests that the reduction would reach the descriptor's $\tilde\Theta(N)$ degree on $N$ unknowns, without the block register, and the ultraspherical descriptor of \Cref{app:descriptor-gap}, which keeps a flat gap on regular problems, is consistent with that. The expectation is untested. The bound concerns a square system with conditions at the ends of the interval rather than a least-squares residual whose rows the conversions weight (\Cref{app:ultraspherical}), and the reduction's encoding with variable coefficients and its gap on singular problems and on two axes were not measured.

Of coupled systems, only first-order systems were run. Complex-valued fields are carried by realification alone; no complex residual was prepared. The tensor layout's native complex encoding is verified but prepares nothing (\Cref{sec:systems-complex}). The thesis presents no test of a lift's divergence that reads the residual alone (\Cref{sec:results-nonlinear}). Wu et al.'s partial differential and nonlinear examples, their Figs~6 and~7, were not reproduced under any hypothesis tested, and the authors were not contacted for clarification (\Cref{app:repro-unreproduced}).
