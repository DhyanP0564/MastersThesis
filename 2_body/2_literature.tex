Quantum algorithms for differential equations promise a cost logarithmic in the number of unknowns, and for the routes with proven costs the promise is conditional. Besides that logarithm, the cost depends on a quantity that the discretisation determines before any circuit is built: the condition number of a linear system, the norm of a generator times the evolution time, or the norm of an encoded Hamiltonian against its gap. For a linearised nonlinear equation, the strength of the nonlinearity against the dissipation decides whether the linearisation converges at all. These quantities typically grow with the resolution, so an accurate discretisation can still be an expensive one, and the choice of discretisation matters as much as the choice of algorithm. Classical spectral numerical analysis has studied how a discretisation sets its conditioning and how to lower it, and the quantum primitives of block encoding and polynomial filtering turn the resulting quantity into a count of queries.

\subsection{Quantum Computation Preliminaries}
\label{sec:bg-qc}

%   post-selection; queries x gates per query, and qubits; the tilde convention; the

% TRAPS  "encode both 0 and 1 simultaneously"; circuit diagrams as Feynman diagrams; a

We use the circuit model of Nielsen and Chuang \cite{nielsenchuang2010}. The state of $n$ qubits is a unit vector in $\mathbb{C}^N$, $N=2^n$, and a vector $v\in\mathbb{C}^N$ is \emph{amplitude-encoded} as $\ket{v}=v/\|v\|$. The encoding stores only the direction of $v$, so its length must be carried separately (for a differential equation, the scale $\eta$ of \Cref{sec:pihm-model}), and it gives access to $v$ only through measurement, which returns the index $j$ with probability $|v_j|^2/\|v\|^2$; recovering all $N$ entries takes at least of order $N$ repetitions. Circuits of unitary gates act on these states, and a non-unitary operation is applied by acting on the system together with \emph{ancilla} qubits prepared in $\ket{0}$ and keeping only the outcomes in which they are measured in $\ket{0}$ again. This \emph{post-selection} succeeds with a probability that enters the cost; every non-unitary matrix of the thesis, from a residual to a filter, acts on a state this way.

An algorithm that accesses a matrix only through a circuit encoding it is costed in \emph{queries}, the uses of that circuit, and its gate count is the number of queries times the gates per query, on a register of system and ancilla qubits. The asymptotic results reviewed below, and the filter degree of \Cref{eq:degree}, count queries; the gates per query belong to the encoding circuit. A tilde on $\mathcal O$, $\Omega$ or $\Theta$ suppresses factors logarithmic in $N$ and in $1/\varepsilon$, and a cost polynomial in $n=\log_2N$ is called poly-logarithmic.

Every cost in the thesis is that of a fault-tolerant machine, whose logical qubits and gates are protected by error correction. Current \gls{nisq} processors have no error correction, and noise limits the depth of a reliable circuit \cite{preskill2018nisq}; the variational algorithms below target them. A filter applies its block encoding as many times in sequence as its degree, which grows with the resolution, so its feasibility is judged against fault-tolerant resource estimates (\Cref{sec:disc-feasibility}) and NISQ hardware is out of scope.

\paragraph{Linear Systems.}
\label{sec:bg-de-linear}
% LAND: the route in one sentence; HHL (cost, kappa lower bound) -> Berry 2014 -> BCOW 2017 ->
%   Childs-Liu 2020 -> CLO 2021 -> Costa 2022 -> Jennings et al.; the ODE solvers take
%   first-order systems (the FOSLS point of sec:bg-fosls). Strengths: generality, proven costs,
%   log N, log 1/eps. Weaknesses: kappa grows with resolution and T; readout; Montanaro-Pallister.

The first route discretises the equation, in time and where needed in space, into one linear system whose solution holds the trajectory, and solves that system with a quantum linear-systems algorithm. The \gls{hhl} algorithm prepares a state proportional to $A^{-1}b$ for an $s$-sparse $N\times N$ matrix of condition number $\kappa$ in time $\tilde{\mathcal O}(s^2\kappa^2\log N/\varepsilon)$, and shows that time $\kappa^{1-\delta}$ for any $\delta>0$ would imply BQP $=$ PSPACE \cite{harrow2009hhl}. The first differential-equation solvers built on it differ in how they discretise. Berry used a linear multistep method, at a cost polynomial in $1/\varepsilon$ \cite{berry2014highorder}, and Berry, Childs, Ostrander and Wang a truncated Taylor series, at a cost polynomial in $\log(1/\varepsilon)$ \cite{berry2017linearode}. Childs and Liu discretised time with Chebyshev collocation, which converges fast but gives a dense collocation matrix, and bound its condition number by a polynomial of degree $3.5$ in the number of nodes \cite{childsliu2020spectral}. Childs, Liu and Ostrander extended finite-difference and spectral discretisations to \glspl{pde} at the same dependence on precision \cite{childsliuostrander2021pde}. Costa et al.\ brought the linear-systems solver itself to the optimal $\mathcal O(\kappa\log(1/\varepsilon))$ with a discrete adiabatic theorem \cite{costa2022optimal}. Jennings et al.\ gave the first non-asymptotic costs, constant factors included, for preparing the history state of any stable linear system, with complexity $\mathcal O(T^{1/2})$ in the evolution time, a fast-forwarding that classical stability permits \cite{jennings2023cost}.

The ordinary-equation solvers take the equation as a first-order system $x'=Ax+b$, reducing any higher order before discretising, so the condition number in their cost is that of a first-order operator. Any linear equation whose discretised operator can be accessed efficiently is then solved at a proven cost logarithmic in $N$ and, from Berry et al.\ on, in $1/\varepsilon$. That cost runs through $\kappa$, which the discretisation sets and which grows with the resolution and with the evolution time (\Cref{sec:bg-spectral}). Learning all $N$ amplitudes of the output takes at least of order $N$ repetitions, and for equations in a fixed number of dimensions with smooth solutions the advantage over the best classical methods is at most polynomial \cite{montanaro2016fem}.

\paragraph{Hamiltonian Simulation and Schrödingerisation.}
\label{sec:bg-de-hamsim}
% LAND: x' = -iHx is Hamiltonian simulation (qubitisation, O(alpha T + log 1/eps)); LCHS and
%   Schroedingerisation extend it to non-unitary linear dynamics. Strengths: no linear system,
%   no kappa; inherits an optimal primitive; LCHS's optimal state-preparation cost. Weaknesses:
%   linear only; ||A|| grows with resolution; final state only; repetitions grow with the
%   decay ||x(0)||/||x(T)||; LCHS needs a PSD dissipative part.

A linear equation $x'=-\mathrm{i}Hx$ with Hermitian $H$ is a Schrödinger equation, and integrating it to time $T$ is Hamiltonian simulation, which qubitisation performs with $\mathcal O(\alpha T+\log(1/\varepsilon))$ queries to a block encoding of $H$ at subnormalisation $\alpha$, an optimal dependence on both \cite{lowchuang2017qsp,lowchuang2019qubitization}. Two constructions carry non-unitary linear dynamics $x'=-Ax$ to this primitive. The linear combination of Hamiltonian simulations writes the propagator of a dissipative system as a weighted integral of unitary evolutions and implements it as a \gls{lcu}, without dilating the problem into a larger linear system and at optimal state-preparation cost \cite{anliulin2023lchs}. Schrödingerisation maps a linear ordinary or partial differential equation to a Schrödinger equation in one more dimension by a warped phase transformation, and recovers the solution from the auxiliary variable \cite{jinliuyu2023schrodingerisation}. Neither solves a linear system, so neither has a condition number in its cost: the cost grows with $\|A\|T$, the norm of the generator times the evolution time. The route is linear, and its cost also grows with the resolution, since the norm of a discretised spatial operator grows with the number of unknowns, as the square of the resolution for a second derivative. It returns the state at the final time only, and recovering it from a dissipative evolution needs a number of repetitions that grows with the decay $\|x(0)\|/\|x(T)\|$ of the solution's norm. The linear combination, as stated, also requires the dissipative part of $A$, its Hermitian part, to be positive semi-definite, which restricts it to stable dynamics.

\paragraph{Variational Algorithms.}
\label{sec:bg-de-variational}
% LAND: Lubasch (copies; NLS); DQC (expectation-value model, derivatives by parameter shift on
%   the feature map, PINN loss); ground states found variationally: VQLS's H_G (the same Gram
%   structure as the PIHM), the Feynman-Kitaev clock (Barison) and space-time Burgers (Pool):
%   antecedents of the time axis of sec:systems-fields. Strengths: shallow circuits, NISQ;
%   nonlinearity handled directly. Weaknesses: no guarantees; barren plateaus; sampled costs.

A variational algorithm represents the solution by a parametrised circuit and minimises a cost built from the equation with a classical optimiser. Lubasch et al.\ evaluate a nonlinear cost on several copies of the state, as demonstrated on the nonlinear Schrödinger equation \cite{lubasch2020variational}. The differentiable quantum circuits of Kyriienko, Paine and Elfving represent the solution as the expectation value of a circuit with input $x$, differentiate it with respect to $x$ by parameter shifts on the feature map, and train it on the residual of the equation and its data \cite{kyriienko2021differentiable}, the loss of a \gls{pinn} \cite{raissi2019pinn}. The variational linear solver finds a ground state the same way, minimising $\braket{x|A^\dagger(I-\ket{b}\bra{b})A|x}$, whose zero-energy ground state solves $Ax\propto b$ \cite{bravoprieto2023vqls}; encoding time in a register, as the Feynman--Kitaev clock does, puts a whole trajectory in one ground state \cite{barison2022variational}, and a space--time encoding of this kind has given the evolution of Burgers' equation from a single ground-state computation \cite{pool2024nonlinear}. The route's strengths are its circuits, shallow enough for NISQ devices, and its reach: a nonlinear term enters the cost directly, with no linearisation. Its weakness is that nothing bounds the number of optimisation steps or guarantees the optimum. The gradients of a sufficiently random parametrised circuit vanish exponentially in the number of qubits, the barren plateau \cite{mcclean2018barren}, and each evaluation of the cost is a sampled expectation value.

\paragraph{Ground-State Preparation.}
\label{sec:bg-de-ground}
% LAND: the null vector of a PSD Hamiltonian, prepared without optimisation (Subasi-Somma-
%   Orsucci; An-Lin; Costa; Lin-Tong's H_1 and its 1/kappa gap; the Chebyshev filter).
%   Strengths: proven cost, no training; the cost law separates encoding from spectrum
%   (eq:degree); log 1/eps. Weaknesses: the gap carries the conditioning; a known gap and an
%   initial overlap are assumed. Then ONE sentence: the PIHM belongs to this route (Ch. 3).

The last route builds a positive semi-definite Hamiltonian whose null vector is the solution and prepares that vector without optimisation. Subaşı, Somma and Orsucci do so for a linear system by randomised adiabatic evolution at cost $\mathcal O(\kappa\log\kappa/\varepsilon)$ \cite{subasi2019adiabatic}, An and Lin by a time-optimal adiabatic schedule \cite{anlin2022timeoptimal}, and Costa et al.\ by a discrete adiabatic walk \cite{costa2022optimal}. In Lin and Tong's form \cite{lintong2020filtering}, for positive-definite $A$ with $\|A\|=1$ and $Q_b=I-\ket{b}\bra{b}$, the Hamiltonian with off-diagonal blocks $AQ_b$ and $Q_bA$ has $\ket{0}\ket{x}$ in its null space beside $\ket{1}\ket{b}$, which a measurement of the first qubit removes; the rest of its spectrum lies at a distance of order $1/\kappa$ from zero, and an optimal Chebyshev filter projects onto the null space. The route's strength is a proven cost with nothing to train, in a form that separates the circuit from the spectrum: a filter's degree depends on the Hamiltonian only through the subnormalisation of its block encoding and its gap, and its error falls exponentially with the degree (\Cref{eq:degree}). Its weakness is that the gap carries the problem's conditioning, of order $1/\kappa$ for a linear system, so the cost grows with $\kappa$ here too. A filter also needs the gap, or a bound on it, in advance, and an initial state whose overlap with the solution does not vanish. The physics-informed effective Hamiltonian of Wu et al.\ belongs to this route: it builds the Hamiltonian from a Chebyshev discretisation of the equation and its data and prepares the ground state with a filter (\Cref{sec:pihm}).

\paragraph{Critical Assessment.}
\label{sec:bg-de-assessment}
% LAND: the verdict across routes, read off T1: (1) the routes with proven costs depend on a
%   quantity the discretisation and encoding determine, the variational route on none; (2) output is a state, gains at most polynomial at fixed
%   dimension, so the thesis compares quantum methods with one another and claims no classical
%   advantage; (3) guarantees uneven; (4) only the variational route is nonlinear without a
%   linearisation (sec:bg-carleman).

The routes of \Cref{tab:de-approaches} differ most in their guarantees. The linear-systems, Hamiltonian-simulation and ground-state routes have proven costs, each running through a property of the discretised problem ($\kappa$, $\|A\|T$, or the encoded norm against the gap) that typically grows with the resolution, and the variational route has no bound at all. Only the variational route takes a nonlinear equation as it stands; the others are linear and reach a nonlinear equation through a linearisation such as Carleman's, which needs dissipation to dominate (\Cref{sec:bg-carleman}). Every route returns a quantum state, so its advantage holds only where a few properties of the solution suffice \cite{aaronson2015fineprint,williams2024readout}, and is at most polynomial in a fixed, low number of dimensions \cite{montanaro2016fem}. The thesis therefore compares quantum methods with one another, at equal accuracy, in queries and gates, and claims no advantage over classical solvers.

\paragraph{Chebyshev Discretisation and Differentiation.}
\label{sec:bg-chebyshev}
% LAND: spectral accuracy; Galerkin, tau, collocation; the PIHM as a least-squares relative of
%   the tau method (not "collocation", T-37); differentiation dense, strictly upper triangular,
%   N^2 per derivative; tau/collocation conditioning N^{2k} (Olver-Townsend, §4).

A function analytic on $[-1,1]$ has a Chebyshev series $f=\sum_kb_kT_k$ with geometrically decaying coefficients, so the $N$-term truncation converges faster than any power of $N$ \cite{trefethen2013approximation}. A differential equation becomes a finite system by Galerkin projection onto the basis, by the tau method, which projects but replaces the highest-degree rows by the boundary conditions, or by collocation at a set of nodes \cite{canuto2006spectral,boyd2001Chebyshev}. Wu et al.'s construction is closest to the tau method, with the equation acting on the coefficients and the data imposed as rows at points, but its system is overdetermined and solved by least squares (\Cref{sec:pihm-residual}).

Differentiation in coefficient space is exact on polynomials of degree below $N$ and dense, because the derivative of $T_p$ contains every $T_k$ of the opposite parity below it: the matrix is upper triangular, about a quarter of its entries are non-zero, and its norm grows as $N^2$ (\Cref{sec:standard-operators}). An operator of order $k$ built from its powers grows as $N^{2k}$, and so, typically, does the condition number of a tau or collocation discretisation \cite{olver2013,canuto2006spectral}.

\paragraph{Ultraspherical Method.}
\label{sec:bg-ultraspherical}
% LAND: T_k' = k C^(1)_{k-1}; D (one superdiagonal) and S (two diagonals); raising the basis
%   per derivative; almost banded; kappa linear in the truncation, bounded after a diagonal
%   preconditioner (2-norm for Dirichlet). The descriptor's Bt, Dt are its first pair (App. A);
%   block rescaling takes the preconditioner's place. VOLUNTEER: the full ultraspherical reduction
%   (banded on N unknowns, no block register) is not built or compared (app:ultraspherical);
%   it is the natural next comparison (sec:limitations).
% 2026-10-16 (GeminiReviewPlan.md row 2, Prompt 4): Olver-Townsend's hypotheses checked against
%   the paper (§4.1, Lemma 4.4: a_N normalised to 1, K = N conditions, (B;L) invertible; 2-norm
%   for Dirichlet; kappa ~ n without the preconditioner) and named here; the unbuilt reduction
%   now carries §9.5's hedged expectation ("suggests, untested").

Olver and Townsend make differentiation banded by letting the derivative live in another basis \cite{olver2013}. Since $T_k'=k\,C^{(1)}_{k-1}$, with $C^{(1)}_k=U_k$ the Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind, differentiation from Chebyshev to $C^{(1)}$ coefficients is a single superdiagonal, and conversion of a series' own coefficients to $C^{(1)}$ has two non-zero diagonals. Raising the basis one ultraspherical (Gegenbauer) level $C^{(\lambda)}$ per derivative gives, for an equation of order $k$ with polynomial coefficients, a leading coefficient that does not vanish and $k$ boundary conditions, an operator banded apart from its boundary rows, whose condition number grows linearly with the truncation and is bounded after a diagonal preconditioner, in the 2-norm for Dirichlet conditions \cite{olver2013}. The dense differentiation matrix we use factorises as $\G=\Bt^{-1}\Dt$, with $\Bt$ and $\Dt$ the method's first conversion and differentiation operators up to a factor of two (\Cref{app:ultraspherical}); the descriptor form of \Cref{sec:descriptor} keeps the two factors, and its block rescaling takes the place of the preconditioner.

Olver and Townsend eliminate the lower derivatives and solve one banded system for the solution's coefficients, whereas the descriptor keeps every derivative as an unknown in the Chebyshev basis, tied to the next by the same pair $(\Bt,\Dt)$. That full reduction is not compared here (\Cref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{First-Order System Least Squares.}
\label{sec:bg-fosls}
% LAND: LS of a 2nd-order operator: O(h^-4) against Galerkin's O(h^-2) (Bochev-Gunzburger 1998,
%   §2.2); FOSLS (Cai et al. 1994): derivatives as unknowns, O(h^-2), nearly decoupled variables,
%   block preconditioners (Bochev-Gunzburger 1998 §2.3; 2009). The PIHM IS a least-squares
%   functional: the standard form is the high-order LS method, the descriptor is FOSLS in a
%   spectral basis. The filter's degree follows R's conditioning (sec:standard-hamiltonian); first order
%   helps it all the same. The quantum ODE solvers already take first-order systems. The
%   increment is stated in sec:bg-synthesis, not here.

The normal equations of a least-squares method square the residual operator, so high order compounds the conditioning: least squares applied directly to a second-order elliptic equation needs continuously differentiable finite elements and gives condition numbers of order $h^{-4}$ in the mesh width $h$, against $h^{-2}$ for a Galerkin method \cite{bochevgunzburger1998review}. \Gls{fosls} introduces derivatives as unknowns, such as a flux $v=\nabla\phi$, and minimises the residuals of the resulting first-order system \cite{cai1994fosls}. Where the functional is equivalent to a product of $H^1$ norms, the condition numbers return to order $h^{-2}$ and the unknowns nearly decouple, so block-diagonal preconditioners are effective \cite{bochevgunzburger1998review,bochev2009lsfem}.

The physics-informed Hamiltonian is such a functional. Stacking the operators of the equation and of its data into one residual operator $R$ gives $H=R^\dagger R$, whose energy $\|R\psi\|^2$ is the squared residual of the equation and its data (\Cref{sec:standard-form}). Eliminating every derivative through $\G$, as Wu et al.\ do, is the high-order least-squares method; carrying the derivatives as unknowns is FOSLS in a spectral basis. The quantum ordinary-equation solvers above already work with first-order systems, and the physics-informed construction departs from them in eliminating the derivatives into one dense operator. The degree of a filter applied through a block encoding of $R$ follows the conditioning of $R$ (\Cref{sec:bg-filtering}); the squaring falls only on an eigensolver acting on $H$. A first-order formulation lowers that conditioning, because each block of $R$ then carries one derivative instead of $k$ (\Cref{sec:standard-hamiltonian}).

\paragraph{Fourier--Galerkin Truncation.}
\label{sec:bg-fourier}
% LAND: Fourier diagonalises differentiation; quadratic terms as convolutions; Galerkin
%   truncation exact on the retained modes; pseudo-spectral evaluation aliases unless dealiased;
%   the fluids (sec:fluids) use the Galerkin truncation on real modes, which the lift needs.

On a periodic domain the Fourier basis diagonalises differentiation, $\partial_xe^{\mathrm{i}kx}=\mathrm{i}k\,e^{\mathrm{i}kx}$, so linear terms act mode by mode and a quadratic term becomes a convolution of coefficients \cite{canuto2006spectral}. A Galerkin truncation keeps exactly the convolution terms whose output lies in the retained set of modes; a pseudo-spectral evaluation on a grid aliases the discarded terms onto retained modes unless it is dealiased \cite{canuto2006spectral}. The fluids of \Cref{sec:fluids} use Galerkin truncations on real Fourier modes, so their quadratic coupling is a fixed matrix of exact coefficients, as the Carleman lift requires; a pseudo-spectral simulation serves only as the reference for the truncation's error.

\paragraph{Carleman Linearisation.}
\label{sec:bg-carleman}
% LAND: the classical embedding (Carleman; Kowalski-Steeb), truncated at K; its quantum use
%   (Leyton-Osborne as the earlier alternative, exponential in time; Liu et al.: R < 1,
%   T^2 q/eps, intractable for R >= sqrt 2; Krovi: log 1/eps, negative log-norm). Strength:
%   linear and exact before truncation, so it composes with any linear route. Weaknesses: R < 1
%   SUFFICIENT and worst-case (rho in sec:carleman, measured in sec:results-nonlinear);
%   dissipation must dominate; lift dimension M^K.

Carleman's embedding replaces a polynomial system of ordinary differential equations by an infinite linear system for the monomials of its unknowns \cite{carleman1932,kowalskisteeb1991}. For $u'=F_1u+F_2u^{\otimes2}$ the vector $(u,u^{\otimes2},u^{\otimes3},\dots)$ obeys a linear equation in which each level couples only to itself and to the next, and truncation at order $K$ leaves a finite linear system whose first level approximates $u$. Its use in quantum algorithms followed an earlier approach of Leyton and Osborne, which transforms the amplitudes of many copies of the state nonlinearly, at resources exponential in the integration time \cite{leyton2008nonlinear}. Liu et al.\ solve the truncated Carleman system with a quantum linear-systems algorithm for dissipative quadratic equations in $M$ variables, at cost $T^2q\,\mathrm{poly}(\log T,\log M,\log1/\varepsilon)/\varepsilon$, with $q$ the decay of the solution, when the ratio $R$ of nonlinearity and forcing to dissipation satisfies $R<1$, and prove the problem intractable in the worst case for $R\ge\sqrt2$ \cite{liu2021carleman}; Krovi makes the dependence on precision logarithmic and relaxes the normality of the linear part to a negative log-norm \cite{krovi2023improved}.

The lift is linear and exact before truncation, so a nonlinear equation inherits the guarantees of whichever linear route solves the truncated system, and the dimension $M^K$ of the lift fits in about $K\log_2M$ qubits. Its weakness is the truncation. The condition $R<1$ bounds the truncation error uniformly over states, so it is sufficient and not necessary: a lift may converge for $R>1$, which \Cref{sec:carleman} (writing the ratio $\rho$) measures and \Cref{sec:results-nonlinear} reports. Dissipation must still dominate, since the truncation is controlled only where the nonlinearity is weak against the damping, and the lift's dimension, of order $M^K$, enters the cost of every route that solves it.

\paragraph{Block Encodings and Linear Combinations of Unitaries.}
\label{sec:bg-block-encoding}
% LAND: the (alpha, m, eps) definition (numbered: re-invoked throughout); alpha >= ||A||; LCU
%   (PREPARE/SELECT, alpha = sum |c_t|); the ceil(log2 L) selector count (as Ch. 5 states it);
%   structured encodings; qubitisation, Pi W^k Pi = T_k.

\begin{definition}[Block encoding \cite{gilyen2019qsvt}]
\label{def:block-encoding}
A unitary $U$ on $m+n$ qubits is an $(\alpha,m,\epsilon)$ \emph{block encoding} of an $N\times N$ matrix $A$ if
\begin{equation}
    \bigl\|A-\alpha\,(\bra{0^m}\otimes I)\,U\,(\ket{0^m}\otimes I)\bigr\|\le\epsilon ;
    \label{eq:block-encoding}
\end{equation}
$\alpha$ is its \emph{subnormalisation}, and the encoding is \emph{exact} when $\epsilon=0$.
\end{definition}

Applied to $\ket{0^m}\ket{\psi}$ and post-selected on the ancillas' zero, an exact block encoding applies $A/\alpha$ with success probability $\|A\psi\|^2/\alpha^2$. The block of a unitary has norm at most one, so $\alpha\ge\|A\|$ for every exact block encoding, and the excess $\alpha/\|A\|$ multiplies the cost of every algorithm that uses it. The standard construction is the \gls{lcu} \cite{childs2012lcu}: for $A=\sum_{t<L}c_tU_t$ with unitaries $U_t$, PREPARE loads $\sum_t\sqrt{|c_t|/\alpha}\,\ket{t}$ on $\lceil\log_2L\rceil$ ancilla qubits, SELECT applies $U_t$ controlled on $\ket{t}$, with the phases of the $c_t$ absorbed into the $U_t$, and PREPARE$^\dagger$ returns the ancillas, giving an exact block encoding at $\alpha=\sum_t|c_t|$. An exact combination whose SELECT indexes $L$ distinct operations needs $\lceil\log_2L\rceil$ selector qubits \cite{chakraborty2025qsvt}. Explicit circuits exist for many structured and sparse matrices \cite{camps2024explicit,sunderhauf2024blockencoding}; each carries its own $\alpha$, and the two forms of the residual compared in \Cref{sec:results} differ in both $\|A\|$ and $\alpha/\|A\|$.

If $U$ is Hermitian and block-encodes $H/\alpha$, the walk $W=(2\Pi-I)\,U$, with $\Pi=\ket{0^m}\bra{0^m}\otimes I$, satisfies $\Pi W^k\Pi=T_k(H/\alpha)$: its $k$-th power block-encodes the $k$-th Chebyshev polynomial of the encoded matrix at one query per power \cite{lowchuang2019qubitization}, so a polynomial of degree $d$ in $H/\alpha$ costs $d$ queries once its combination is formed.

\paragraph{Quantum Signal Processing.}
\label{sec:bg-qsp}
% LAND: QSP -> QSVT (definite parity, |P| <= 1, one ancilla) -> the grand unification; the
%   mixed-parity construction for indefinite parity (Wu et al.'s); GQSP (no parity, one signal
%   qubit, |P| <= 1 on the circle); phases from the complementary polynomial (FFT-based, with
%   explicit error guarantees) and the inverse NLFT.

Quantum signal processing interleaves a single-qubit signal rotation with fixed phase rotations to realise a polynomial of the signal \cite{lowchuang2017qsp}. The \gls{qsvt} lifts the construction to block encodings, applying a degree-$d$ polynomial $P$ to the singular values of the encoded matrix with $d$ queries and one further ancilla \cite{gilyen2019qsvt}, and places search, Hamiltonian simulation and linear systems in one framework \cite{martyn2021grand}. Its polynomials are bounded by one on $[-1,1]$ and of definite parity, so a function of indefinite parity, such as the imaginary-time filter $e^{-\beta x}$, is split into even and odd parts, each with its own phase sequence, and the two are summed by a further linear combination at a cost in subnormalisation; Wu et al.\ use this mixed-parity construction (\Cref{sec:pihm-encoding}). \Gls{gqsp} drops the parity constraint \cite{motlagh2024gqsp}. SU(2) rotations on one signal qubit, with the encoded unitary applied controlled on it, realise any $P(z)=\sum_{k\le d}q_kz^k$ with $|P|\le1$ on the unit circle at $d$ queries, and applied to the walk $W$ they give $\sum_kq_kT_k$ of the encoded matrix. The phases follow from a complementary polynomial, computed by a fast-Fourier-transform formula with explicit error guarantees \cite{berntson2025complementary}, through the inverse of the \gls{nlft} to which GQSP is equivalent \cite{laneve2025gqspnlft,nlfft2025su2}.

\paragraph{Ground-State Preparation by Filtering.}
\label{sec:bg-filtering}
% LAND: imaginary time and inverse iteration as filters; Lin-Tong's eigenstate filter (interior
%   eigenvalue, degree O(alpha/Delta log 1/eps), optimal on [-1,-D] u [D,1]) and ground-state
%   algorithm (sign function, O(alpha/(gamma Delta) log 1/eps); essentially optimal in gamma and
%   Delta); the known-gap assumption. THEN the edge: eq:degree, the square root from the edge
%   (Chebyshev growth), which for a frustration-free H is Somma-Boixo's amplification (H = R^T R
%   is one exactly when its kernel is exact); a small ground value changes p_G by P_d(x_0)^2,
%   never the degree (2026-10-16, GeminiReviewPlan row 1; no degradation formula), and the hedge (polynomial filters given the edge; no
%   all-algorithm floor, sec:disc-complexity). Overlap and amplitude amplification. Readout as a
%   closing paragraph (was §2.7).
% HOME OF eq:degree: the two paragraphs around the display are the ONLY statement of the formula
%   and of alpha_H >= ||H||; Chs 3-7 cite \Cref{eq:degree} and do not restate either.

A filter is a function of $H$ that keeps the ground eigencomponent of an initial state $\psi_{\mathrm{init}}$ and suppresses the rest. Imaginary-time evolution $e^{-\beta H}$ does so once $\beta$ exceeds the inverse gap \cite{motta2020qite}, and inverse iteration $H^{-k}$ by powers \cite{kyriienko2020inverse}; expanded in polynomials, both are implementable by the transformations above. For an eigenvalue at zero with the rest of the spectrum in $[-1,-\Delta]\cup[\Delta,1]$, Lin and Tong's eigenstate filter, a Chebyshev polynomial in $x^2$, has the least maximum on that set among polynomials of its degree equal to one at zero, and needs degree $\mathcal O(\Delta^{-1}\log(1/\varepsilon))$ \cite{lintong2020filtering}. Their ground-state algorithm approximates a shifted sign function, given a bound that separates the ground energy from the rest of the spectrum, with $\mathcal O\bigl((\alpha/(\gamma\Delta))\log(1/\varepsilon)\bigr)$ queries for subnormalisation $\alpha$, gap $\Delta$ and overlap $\gamma=|\braket{\psi_0|\psi_{\mathrm{init}}}|$ with the ground state; they argue, by reduction from unstructured search, that its dependence on $\gamma$ and $\Delta$ is essentially optimal \cite{lintong2020groundstate}. Both assume the gap, or bounds that imply it, known in advance, a standard assumption that we share.

Both place the ground eigenvalue inside the encoded spectrum; at its edge the degree falls quadratically. Let $H\succeq0$ be block-encoded with its spectrum mapped into $[-1,1]$ and the ground value at the lower edge, as by $2H/\alpha_H-I$, with gap $\Delta=\lambda_1-\lambda_0$ and a ground value small against $\Delta$. The excited spectrum then lies at a distance of order $\Delta/\alpha_H$ from the ground value, and the Chebyshev polynomial of that interval, the edge counterpart of Lin and Tong's filter, has the least maximum on it relative to its value at the ground state among polynomials of its degree \cite{borweinerdelyi1995} (\Cref{app:minimax-filter}). Suppressing the excited spectrum to $\varepsilon$ relative to the ground state needs the degree
\begin{equation}
    d = \Theta\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_H}{\Delta}}\,\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),
    \qquad
    \alpha_H = \alpha_R^2 .
    \label{eq:degree}
\end{equation}
For frustration-free Hamiltonians, sums of positive terms that all vanish on the ground state, the square root is Somma and Boixo's spectral gap amplification \cite{somma2013gap}, and $H=R^\dagger R$ is one exactly when its kernel is exact. A small non-zero ground value leaves the degree unchanged, since the degree is set by the gap, and costs only the square of the filter's value at the ground state, a factor close to one, in the probability of success (\Cref{app:minimax-filter}). Here $\alpha_R$ is the subnormalisation of a block encoding of $R$, from which one reflection block-encodes $H$ at $\alpha_H=\alpha_R^2$; the walk's eigenphases are set by the singular values of $R/\alpha_R$, and the gap the filter resolves is of order $\sigma_1/\alpha_R=(\Delta/\alpha_H)^{1/2}$ (\Cref{sec:pipeline-walk}).

The degree $d$ counts the queries of one filtered preparation before amplification. The circuit enters only through $\alpha_H\ge\|H\|$, so with this filter no encoding brings the degree below order $\sqrt{\|H\|/\Delta}\,\log(1/\varepsilon)$. With $\alpha_H=\alpha_R^2$ the degree is linear in $\alpha_R$; for an exact kernel, whose gap is the square of the smallest non-zero singular value of $R$, $\sqrt{\alpha_H/\Delta}$ is at least the ratio of the largest singular value of $R$ to that one, the residual's effective condition number. The bound holds for polynomial filters given the edge and the gap, among which Chebyshev's inequality makes the degree least; over all algorithms the thesis claims no more than Lin and Tong's optimality supports (\Cref{sec:disc-complexity}).

Post-selection succeeds with probability of order $\gamma^2$, and amplitude amplification raises it to near one with $\mathcal O(1/\gamma)$ repetitions \cite{brassard2000amplitude}, so a preparation costs of order $d/\gamma$ queries; an answer-independent initial state with non-vanishing overlap is part of the method (\Cref{sec:pipeline-filter}).

\paragraph{Readout.} An overlap or expectation value is estimated to additive error $\delta$ by a Hadamard or swap test with $\mathcal O(1/\delta^2)$ repetitions, or with $\mathcal O(1/\delta)$ by amplitude estimation, including variants without phase estimation \cite{brassard2000amplitude,suzuki2020amplitude,grinko2021iterative}; a full field needs of order $N$ such estimates, the readout problem of quantum differential-equation algorithms \cite{williams2024readout,nishi2025overlap}. Wu et al.\ read the field point by point with an interferometric overlap measurement (\Cref{sec:pihm-encoding}). We decode the field classically from the prepared state, which our classical computations make available (\Cref{sec:pipeline-readout}), and leave readout unaddressed, following the convention of every route of \Cref{tab:de-approaches}.

\subsection{Synthesis}
\label{sec:bg-synthesis}
% ~0.4 pp. Where the PIHM sits; the increment over the two antecedents (moved here from
%   sec:bg-spectral 2026-10-16); the hand-off to Ch. 3 "on the same attributed footing". The
%   questions themselves are posed at the END of Ch. 3 (sec:pihm-assessment), not here.

The physics-informed effective Hamiltonian is a ground-state route, so its cost follows \Cref{eq:degree}: the square root of an encoded Hamiltonian's norm over its gap. Its Hamiltonian is the least-squares functional of a physics-informed loss on a Chebyshev discretisation, which gives spectral accuracy together with the conditioning of dense differentiation. The authors name the behaviour of the gap and the cost of differentiation as open questions; in the terms of \Cref{eq:degree}, the first is the degree's denominator and, as \Cref{sec:pihm-assessment} argues, the second is its numerator.

Classical numerical analysis has met both difficulties. The ultraspherical method replaces the dense differentiation matrix by banded factors \cite{olver2013}, and first-order-system least squares avoids the squared conditioning of a high-order least-squares functional by carrying the derivatives as unknowns \cite{cai1994fosls}. Both were developed for classical solvers, where conditioning limits accuracy and iteration counts. In a block-encoded Hamiltonian the same conditioning is paid for in queries, yet the construction of Wu et al.\ uses neither remedy, and no work reviewed here carries either to it.

The thesis makes that transfer. Its descriptor form keeps the banded factors and carries the derivatives as unknowns, and the Hamiltonian it gives has the kernel of the form it replaces, so the prepared state is the same solution. The banded residual is encoded as one linear combination of unitaries with $\mathcal O(\log n)$ ancilla qubits. The reformulation has a price: the carried derivatives enter the Hamiltonian's norm and erode its gap, which a block rescaling restores on one axis without ancilla, though not on two. What the reformulation does to the filter's degree, as built and with an ideal encoding, is the measurement on which the contribution rests (\Cref{sec:descriptor,sec:results-scaling}). The next chapter presents the construction of Wu et al.\ and judges it against the two difficulties before the transfer is made.
