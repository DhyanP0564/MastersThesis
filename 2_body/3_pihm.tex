This chapter presents and evaluates the physics-informed effective Hamiltonian method of Wu, Paine, Philip, Gentile and Kyriienko \cite{wu2025pihm}. \Cref{sec:pihm-model,sec:pihm-residual,sec:pihm-encoding} outline the paper's methodology. We provide our critical assessment in \Cref{sec:pihm-doubled,sec:pihm-assessment}, and report our reproduction of results in \Cref{sec:results-reproduction}.

The method turns a differential equation into a ground-state problem. The unknown function is expanded in Chebyshev polynomials and its coefficient vector is stored as the amplitudes of an $n$-qubit state $\ket{\psi}$, so that the function's value at any point is an overlap of $\ket{\psi}$ with a known feature state. The differential equation and each of its boundary or initial conditions are then written as matrix operators that annihilate $\ket{\psi}$ when it holds the solution's coefficients. The paper's introductory example $f''+f=0$ becomes $(\G^{2}+I)\ket{\psi}=0$, where $\G$ is the matrix that differentiates a Chebyshev series. Finally, a positive semi-definite matrix operator $H$, which we label as the effective Hamiltonian, is created from summing the individual gram matrices of all operators. The ground state of the Hamiltonian is the solution. With this construction, solving the equation becomes preparing that ground state, which needs a block encoding of $H$ and a filter that isolates its lowest eigenvector, after which the field is read back through the same feature map. The construction descends from differentiable quantum circuits \cite{kyriienko2021differentiable} through their Chebyshev latent space \cite{paine2023physicsinformed}.

\subsection{The Chebyshev Latent Space Model}
\label{sec:pihm-model}
% ~0.6 pp
% LAND: the normalised basis; f_q(x) = <tau(x)|psi>; the scale eta; raw tau in
%   the rank-one rows. The closed form eta_e = ||b||^2 = (N/pi) int f^2/sqrt(1-x^2)
%   is OURS, so it is stated in §8.2 (eq:eta-e), not here.

Any sufficiently regular function $f$ on $[-1,1]$ (Lipschitz continuity suffices) is an infinite sum of Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind, $f(x)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\hat f_\ell T_\ell(x)$, where the $T_\ell$ are orthogonal with respect to the weight $(1-x^2)^{-1/2}$ \cite{trefethen2013approximation}. We truncate this sum at degree $N-1$, with $N=2^n$ for an $n$-qubit register, so that $N$ coefficients are stored and every polynomial of degree below $N$ is represented exactly.

The coefficients are carried by a quantum state through the quantum Chebyshev state \cite{wu2025pihm}
\begin{equation}
    \ket{\tau(x)}_n = \frac{1}{2^{n/2}}T_0(x)\ket{0}^{\otimes n} + \frac{1}{2^{(n-1)/2}}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1} T_k(x)\ket{k}.
    \label{eq:pihm-chebyshev-feature-map}
\end{equation}
The weights $2^{-n/2}$ and $2^{-(n-1)/2}$ normalise the basis: the $N$ states $\ket{\tau(\bar x_j)}_n$ at the Chebyshev nodes $\bar x_j=\cos[\pi(j+\tfrac12)/N]$ are orthonormal, so the map between $\ket{\psi}$ and the model's nodal values is orthogonal. Away from the nodes $\ket{\tau(x)}_n$ is not a unit vector, since $\|\tau(x)\|^2\le 2-1/N$. A circuit prepares the normalised Chebyshev state $\ket{\tilde\tau(x)}_n=\ket{\tau(x)}_n/\|\tau(x)\|$, by an $x$-dependent feature map on one ancilla qubit and $n$ system qubits followed by post-selection on the ancilla, and the paper discards the factor $\|\tau(x)\|$ as absorbed into the scaling factor of the model. Because that factor depends on $x$, we keep $\ket{\tau(x)}_n$ explicit and write the model with it throughout.

The model is the overlap
\begin{equation}
    f(x) \approx f_Q(x) = \sqrt{\eta}\,f_q(x), \qquad f_q(x) = \braket{\tau(x)|\psi}_n ,
    \label{eq:pihm-fq}
\end{equation}
following the latent-space formulation of Paine et al.\ \cite{paine2023physicsinformed}. Here $\ket{\psi}=b/\|b\|$ is the unit vector of the normalised coefficients $b_0 = 2^{n/2}\hat f_0$ and $b_\ell = 2^{(n-1)/2}\hat f_\ell$ for $1\le\ell<N$, so that $\braket{\tau(x)|b}$ is the truncated series; $f_q$ is the unit-vector model and $f_Q$ the physical field. A unit vector cannot carry the norm of the coefficients, so that norm is split off as the real scale factor $\sqrt{\eta}$, whose sign and magnitude are fixed by the regular datum (\Cref{eq:pihm-regular}); the ideal value of $\eta$, the squared norm $\|b\|^2$, is given in \Cref{eq:eta-e}.

Invariant constraints ($C\ket{\psi}=0$) are unchanged when $\ket{\psi}$ is rescaled, so the Hamiltonian built from them determines only the direction of $\ket{\psi}$; the same holds for the equation's own row, which is linear in $\ket{\psi}$ even with a source term (\Cref{sec:pihm-residual}). The scale $\sqrt{\eta}$ is recovered from a single known value of the solution after the ground state has been prepared.

\label{sec:pihm-residual}

The paper writes each piece of information about the solution as a matrix $C$ with $C\ket{\psi}=0$ when $\ket{\psi}$ holds the solution's coefficients \cite{wu2025pihm}. This is possible because the components of $\bra{\tau(x)}$ are linearly independent functions of $x$. A condition that holds at every $x\in[-1,1]$ becomes $\bra{\tau(x)}C\ket{\psi}=0$ for every $x$ once the model is substituted, and since no non-zero combination of the components vanishes identically, this is equivalent to the finite equation $C\ket{\psi}=0$: the dependence on $x$ drops out. The one obstacle is a term in which $x$ multiplies the model, as in $xf''$, because the factor $x$ is not a matrix acting on $\ket{\psi}$; the lifting operators below move it into the feature vector instead.

Differentiation maps the coefficients of a Chebyshev series linearly onto those of its derivative \cite{trefethen2000spectral}. The map is dense because $T_p' = 2p\sum T_k$, summed over the $k<p$ of opposite parity to $p$ (with weight $p$ at $k=0$): a single high-degree polynomial differentiates into every lower polynomial of the other parity. In the normalised basis of \Cref{eq:pihm-chebyshev-feature-map} the map is the $N \times N$ matrix
\begin{equation}
    [\G]_{k,p} =
    \begin{cases}
        2p, & p > k \ge 1,\ p-k\ \text{odd}, \\
        \sqrt{2}\,p, & k = 0,\ p\ \text{odd}, \\
        0, & \text{otherwise},
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:pihm-G-entries}
\end{equation}
with $f_q'(x) = \braket{\tau(x)|\G|\psi}_n$ and the $m$-th derivative given by $\G^{m}$. Here $\G$ acts on coefficient vectors; Wu et al.\ print the same matrix as $\G_n^{\top}$. It is strictly upper triangular, and its row $k$ is non-zero at every column $p>k$ of the other parity, so the lowest coefficient of a derivative depends on half the coefficients of the function. $\G$ therefore has $\Theta(N^2)$ non-zero entries, and $\|\G\|_2 = \Theta(N^2)$. Its fill-in at $N=4$ is written out in \Cref{app:cheb}, beside the banded factors that \Cref{sec:descriptor-construction} extracts from the same recurrence.

%   and $\|\G\|_2=\Theta(N^2)$ is the norm of that matrix, not an artefact of the normalised basis. (Exact because a series of degree $<N$ differentiates within the same space.)

For a homogeneous linear equation of order $k$ with constant coefficients, $\sum_{j=0}^k a_j f^{(j)} = 0$, substituting $\G^{j}$ for each derivative gives the residual operator
\begin{equation}
    \Astd = \sum_{j=0}^k a_j\, \G^{j},
    \label{eq:pihm-A}
\end{equation}
which acts on the same $n$ qubits as $\ket{\psi}$; the introductory example gives $\Astd=\G^2+I$. If the solution is a polynomial of degree below $N$, its coefficients satisfy $\Astd\ket{\psi}=0$ exactly.

A variable coefficient cannot be substituted in this way, because the Chebyshev coefficients of a product $a_j(x)f^{(j)}(x)$ are not the products of the factors' coefficients. The paper's remedy is a lifting operator $\M_{x^p}$ that carries a factor $x^p$ across the overlap. It rests on the product-to-sum identity
\begin{equation}
    x^p\, T_k(x) = 2^{-p}\sum_{i=0}^{p}\binom{p}{i}\, T_{|k+p-2i|}(x),
    \label{eq:pihm-product-to-sum}
\end{equation}
the $p$-fold iterate of the rule $T_jT_k = \tfrac12(T_{j+k}+T_{|j-k|})$ on which the nonlinear extension of \Cref{sec:pihm-doubled} also rests. Written in the normalised bases of \Cref{eq:pihm-chebyshev-feature-map}, it gives an operator with
\begin{equation}
    x^p\,\bra{\tau(x)}_n = \bra{\tau(x)}_{n+1}\,\M_{x^p},
    \label{eq:pihm-Mxp}
\end{equation}
whose entries are given in \Cref{app:pihm-residual}. Multiplying a series of degree $N-1$ by $x^p$ raises its degree by $p$, so the product needs more than $N$ coefficients. One extra qubit doubles the register: $\M_{x^p}$ is a $2N\times N$ matrix, exact with no truncation for $p\le N$. At $p=0$ the lift reduces to the embedding $\M_1=\sqrt2\,[\,I;\,0\,]$, the factor $\sqrt2$ being the ratio of the normalisations of $\ket{\tau}_{n+1}$ and $\ket{\tau}_n$. A coefficient with the finite power series $a_j(x) = \sum_p c_{j,p}\,x^p$ (a Maclaurin truncation, if the coefficient is not a polynomial) lifts by linearity to $\M_{a_j} = \sum_p c_{j,p}\, \M_{x^p}$, and the equation row becomes
\begin{equation}
    \Astd = \sum_{j=0}^k \M_{a_j}\, \G^{j}.
    \label{eq:pihm-A-general}
\end{equation}
A constant coefficient has $\M_{a_j} = a_j\M_1$. In the paper an axis is lifted only when it carries a non-constant coefficient or a source, so constant-coefficient equations stay on $n$ qubits (its Figs.~3 and 6); when an axis is lifted, every row on it is lifted too, the data constraints below included, so that all rows act into the same $(n+1)$-qubit register.

An equation of order $k$ has a $k$-dimensional space of solutions, and conditions at chosen points select one. Each condition is built from the rank-one point-evaluation row $\Brow_n(x) = \sqrt{2^n}\,\ket{0}^{\otimes n}\bra{\tau(x)}_n$, which is non-zero only in its first row and satisfies $\bra{\tau(x')}\Brow_n(x)\ket{\psi} = \braket{\tau(x)|\psi}$ for every $x'$: it maps the model to the constant function equal to its value at $x$. The paper distinguishes two kinds of condition by how they behave when $\ket{\psi}$ is rescaled. An \emph{invariant} constraint has a zero target and enters the Hamiltonian as a further row: a Dirichlet condition $f(x_z)=0$ gives $\Brow_n(x_z)\ket{\psi}=0$, and a Neumann condition $f'(x_m)=0$ gives $\Brow_n(x_m)\G\ket{\psi}=0$. A \emph{regular} constraint $f(x_s)=y_s$ has a non-zero target, so it cannot be a homogeneous row; it fixes the scale of \Cref{eq:pihm-fq} once the ground state $\ket{\psi_g}$ is known,
\begin{equation}
    \sqrt{\eta} = \frac{y_s}{\braket{\tau(x_s)|\psi_g}_n},
    \label{eq:pihm-regular}
\end{equation}
which requires a point $x_s$ at which the solution does not vanish. For a homogeneous linear equation this is the regular datum's only role. Where a term of the equation must be tied to the amplitude, the same point supplies the \emph{collapse} row $\Dzero(x_s) = \Brow(x_s)/y_s$, which maps $\ket{\psi}$ to the constant $f_q(x_s)/y_s$, equal to $1/\sqrt{\eta}$ on the solution.

That is the case for a source. The right-hand side $r$ of $\sum_j a_j f^{(j)} = r$ does not scale with $\ket{\psi}$, so it cannot be a matrix acting on $\ket{\psi}$ alone. Multiplying the collapse row by the series of $r$ gives a term that does: on the solution, $\Dzero(x_s)\ket{\psi}$ is the constant $1/\sqrt\eta$, and $f_q=f_Q/\sqrt\eta$ satisfies the equation with right-hand side $r/\sqrt\eta$. A non-polynomial source is expanded as a Maclaurin series truncated at order $\bar p$ ($\bar p = 3, 5, 7$ in the paper's inhomogeneous panels) and lifted like a coefficient, and the equation row becomes
\begin{equation}
    \Astd = \sum_{j=0}^{k} \M_{a_j}\,\G^{j} - \M_r\,\Dzero(x_s),
    \label{eq:pihm-A-source}
\end{equation}
with $\M_r=\sum_{p\le\bar p}c_p\M_{x^p}$ the lift of the truncated series. The truncation is an error of its own, measured in \Cref{sec:results-reproduction}. The same collapse row carries the linear terms of a nonlinear equation in \Cref{sec:pihm-doubled}.

For an equation in two variables the state lives on $2n$ qubits, the feature vector is the product of the two one-dimensional ones, and the derivatives act as $\G\otimes I$ and $I\otimes\G$. The heat equation $\partial_t f - \kappa\,\partial_x^2 f=0$ gives $\Astd = \G\otimes I - \kappa\, I\otimes\G^{2}$, and an invariant constraint on the spatial boundary takes the form $I\otimes\Brow_n(x_z)$. The paper's partial-differential panels take their further invariant constraints from zeros of the known solution, $\partial_x f(t,\pm\tfrac34) = 0$ for the heat equation and $f(\tfrac18,x) = f(\tfrac78,x) = 0$ for the wave equation, and do not impose the initial condition as a constraint: it enters only through the regular datum, which sets the scale.

\begin{definition}[Physics-informed effective Hamiltonian]
\label{def:pihm-hamiltonian}
Collecting the equation row $\Astd$ and every invariant constraint $\Brow_i$,
\begin{equation}
    \Hstd = \Astd^\top \Astd + \sum_i \Brow_i^\top \Brow_i, \qquad \braket{\psi|\Hstd|\psi} = \|\Astd\psi\|^2 + \sum_i \|\Brow_i\psi\|^2.
    \label{eq:pihm-hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
$\Hstd$ is real, symmetric and positive semi-definite, and $\braket{\psi|\Hstd|\psi}=0$ exactly when every constraint holds. The equation is thereby the eigenvalue problem $\Hstd\ket{\psi_g} = \lambda_{\min}\ket{\psi_g}$ with $\lambda_{\min}\approx0$, whose ground state $\ket{\psi_g}$ is the solution.
\end{definition}

The energy of a state is its total squared error against the equation and its data. Writing $R = [\Astd; \Brow_1; \dots]$ for the stacked residual, $\Hstd = R^\top R$, so $\ket{\psi_g}$ is the smallest right singular vector of $R$ and $\lambda_{\min}$ the square of its smallest singular value: the construction is least squares, not an exact kernel. A solution that is a polynomial of degree below $N$, with every coefficient and source represented exactly, annihilates every row and gives $\lambda_{\min} = 0$; the paper's Legendre panels of Figs.~4a and 4b are of this kind, while those with a $\sqrt{1-x^2}$ branch point (Figs.~4c and 4d) are not. Any other solution leaves a truncation residual, $\lambda_{\min}>0$ is small, and the ground state is the best fit within the truncated space. Since an equation of order $k$ has $k$ independent solutions, $k-1$ invariant constraints leave a one-dimensional near-kernel, whose scale the regular datum then fixes; the paper's second-order panels use one invariant constraint each. The worked residual at $N=4$ for the paper's pilot equation and the entries of the lifts are given in \Cref{app:pihm-residual}.

We call this construction, in which every derivative is eliminated into powers of the dense $\G$, the \emph{standard form}, and mark its objects with the subscript ``std''. It is the paper's regime, and the baseline against which \Cref{sec:standard} and the later chapters set the reformulation of \Cref{sec:descriptor}, the descriptor form.

A quantum solver needs three subroutines: a block encoding of every term of the Hamiltonian, a filter that isolates its ground state, and a measurement that reads the field from that state. We describe each as the paper presents it, with the claims it makes; the claims are assessed in \Cref{sec:pihm-assessment}.

\paragraph{Block encodings.} Each term of the Hamiltonian is block-encoded separately (\Cref{sec:bg-block-encoding}). The rank-one rows $\Brow(x)$ and $\Dzero(x_s)$ combine the inverse Chebyshev feature map, which prepares $\ket{\tau(x)}$ from the all-zero state, with the reflection $\hat S_{n+1} = 2\ket{0}\!\bra{0} - I$ in a two-term \gls{lcu} \cite{childs2012lcu}; the encoding of $\Brow$ uses $n+2$ qubits at subnormalisation $\sqrt{2^{n+1}}$, and that of $\Dzero(x_s)$ divides it by $f(x_s)$. The dense $\G$ is also encoded as an LCU, which exploits the structure of \Cref{eq:pihm-G-entries}: the entries grow linearly along each superdiagonal, and two conditioned subroutines load them as rotation amplitudes, with a small error in each entry \cite{wu2025pihm}. The circuit's top-left block is $\G$ divided by the prefactor
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_{\G}^{\mathrm{pub}} = \bigl(2^{n-1} + 2^{n}\bigr)\,\|\G\|_S,
    \qquad
    \|\G\|_S = \max\bigl(\|\G\G^{\top}\|_1,\ \|\G^{\top}\G\|_1\bigr).
    \label{eq:pihm-published-prefactor}
\end{equation}
The paper claims that this encoding needs $2n+3$ qubits; that its gate count is linear in $n$ for each of $\Dzero(x_s)$ and $\G$, and $O(n^3)$ overall once the multi-controlled gates are decomposed; and that the entry error decreases exponentially in $n$, for which no bound is given. The registers, subroutines and angles are given in \Cref{app:published-build,app:ladder}, and the claims are tested in \Cref{sec:standard-published}.

\paragraph{The imaginary-time filter.} The ground state is prepared by quantum imaginary-time evolution (\gls{qite}): $e^{-\beta H}$ multiplies each eigencomponent of an initial state by $e^{-\beta E}$, so that the lowest dominates once $\beta$ exceeds the inverse gap. The paper realises it by \gls{qsvt} \cite{gilyen2019qsvt} on the block-encoded $\tilde H = H/\|H\|_F$, with $\|\cdot\|_F$ the Frobenius norm and phase angles fitted to $e^{-\beta x}$ (the machinery is in \Cref{sec:bg-qsp,sec:bg-filtering}). The evolution time is bounded below by
\begin{equation}
    \beta_{\mathrm{pub}} \ge \frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_2\,(n-1)} ,
    \label{eq:pihm-time-rule}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{\max}$ and $\lambda_2$ are the largest and second-smallest eigenvalues; the paper writes $t$ for $\beta$, calls the bound approximate, and states it for $H$ and $\tilde H$ alike, the ratio being scale-free. With $\lambda_{\min}\approx0$, $\lambda_2$ plays the role of the gap $\Delta$. The paper expects the preparation time to scale as $O(1/\Delta)\,\mathrm{polylog}(1/\varepsilon)$, or as $O(1/\sqrt{\Delta})$ with algorithmic thermalisation, and notes that the gaps are ``problem-dependent and in general are not easy to bound'' \cite{wu2025pihm}. The phase sets, the dilation of $\tilde H$ and the parameters of the paper's examples are given in \Cref{app:paper-filter}.

\paragraph{Readout.} The field is read out by an interferometric overlap measurement \cite{kyriienko2020inverse,scali2023topological}. The model value is a signed overlap, but measuring the all-zero outcome after the inverse feature map returns only its square, so the paper measures that probability twice: once for the prepared state, and once for the state superposed with a reference. The two probabilities give the signed overlap $\braket{\tau(x)|\psi_g}$, and with $\eta$ from \Cref{eq:pihm-regular} the field $f_Q(x)$ at the chosen point; where the solution has one sign, the first probability suffices (Eqs.~36--39 of the paper). The field is therefore reconstructed one point at a time. The identity, in the form this thesis uses, is \Cref{eq:readout-identity}.

\subsection{The Doubled-Space Extension to Nonlinearity}
\label{sec:pihm-doubled}
% ~0.6 pp
% LAND: Psi = psi (x) psi with the fold (eq:pihm-fold); QUADRATIC ONLY (Paine 2026
%   also notes it); the paper's >= 2^{2n-1} zero eigenvalues and its quoted
%   "seeking a degenerate state ... that closely matches the analytical solution".
%   The PROJECTS-rather-than-prepares argument is ASSESSMENT; the formal statement

A nonlinear term such as $(f')^2$ is not linear in $\ket{\psi}$, so no matrix acting on $\ket{\psi}$ represents it. The paper linearises it by doubling the Hilbert space: the unknown becomes $\ket{\Psi} = \ket{\psi}\otimes\ket{\psi}$, and a product of two model functions is the overlap of $\ket{\Psi}$ with a product of two feature vectors \cite{wu2025pihm}. The product-to-sum rule folds that product back into a single Chebyshev series on one extra qubit,
\begin{equation}
    \bra{\tau(x)}_n \otimes \bra{\tau(x)}_n = \bra{\tau(x)}_{n+1}\,\nfold_1 ,
    \qquad
    \nfold_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{2N \times N^2} ,
    \label{eq:pihm-fold}
\end{equation}
so that a quadratic term $f^{(i)}f^{(j)}$ becomes the operator $\nfold_1(\G^i\otimes\G^j)$ on $\ket{\Psi}$. The linear terms are carried to the same space by tensoring them with the collapse row $\Dzero(x_s)$, which supplies the constant factor of one copy, so that the doubled model's scale is $\eta$ rather than $\sqrt{\eta}$. The Hamiltonian is then built from the lifted residual and the data constraints as in \Cref{def:pihm-hamiltonian}, on $2n$ qubits, and the paper demonstrates it on two quadratic ordinary differential equations at $n=2$.

The construction handles a \emph{quadratic} nonlinearity only. A term of degree $p$ needs $p$ copies, a register of dimension $N^p$, folded back by $p-1$ applications of the product rule; Paine's concurrent work makes the same observation \cite{paine2026concurrent}. Even at quadratic order, $\ket{\Psi}$ lives in a space of dimension $N^2 = 2^{2n}$, and the paper reports that the Hamiltonian has \emph{at least} $2^{2n-1}$ degenerate zero-energy states, half of that space. Solving the equation is, in the paper's words, ``converted to that of seeking a degenerate state'' that ``closely matches the analytical solution'' \cite{wu2025pihm}, and the text gives no rule for the selection that does not refer to the known solution.

\paragraph{Assessment: projection, not preparation.} The degeneracy decides what a filter can do. Any filter that is a function of the Hamiltonian acts as a scalar on its zero-energy eigenspace, so it returns the normalised projection of the initial state onto the kernel, and the direction it picks within the kernel is set by the initial state, not by the equation. Nor can the Hamiltonian be modified to single out the solution. A kernel is a subspace, so one that contains $\ket{\psi}\otimes\ket{\psi}$ and $\ket{\phi}\otimes\ket{\phi}$ also contains their sum, which is not a product state unless $\ket{\psi}$ and $\ket{\phi}$ are parallel; no penalty built without knowledge of the solution can therefore make the product structure part of the kernel. Extracting the physical solution from the doubled space is selection within a known kernel, not preparation. The formal statement, with its scope, is \Cref{prop:no-penalty}, and \Cref{sec:nonlinear} builds a preparation without this defect for a restricted but practically important class of equations.

\paragraph{Strengths.} The construction is unified: ordinary and partial differential equations and their data constraints take one least-squares form, and nonlinear terms have a route into it. Its kernel is exact whenever the solution is a polynomial within the truncation. The Chebyshev basis needs no spatial grid, so the model and all its derivatives are available at every $x\in[-1,1]$ from the one prepared state, and the paper reports that every term block-encodes with polynomially many gates \cite{wu2025pihm}. These are real advantages over grid-based formulations, and the framework is worth building on.

\paragraph{Weaknesses in the cost.} The most serious weakness is that the spectrum of the Hamiltonian, not its circuits, fixes the cost. For an equation of order $k$, $\|\G\|_2 = \Theta(N^2)$ gives $\|\Hstd\| = \Theta(N^{4k})$, which is $N^8$ at second order; the exponent doubles because the Hamiltonian is a Gram product, $\|\Hstd\| = \|R\|^2$, the familiar penalty of the normal equations. A filter of degree $d = \Theta(\sqrt{\alpha_H/\Delta}\,\log 1/\varepsilon)$ needs $\alpha_H \ge \|\Hstd\|$ for any block encoding, so where the gap $\Delta$ does not grow with $N$, as is the case on the one-axis problems measured in \Cref{sec:standard-hamiltonian}, the degree is at least $\tilde\Theta(N^{2k})$, which is $\tilde\Theta(N^4)$ at second order, \emph{whatever the block encoding}. The method is not polylogarithmic in $N$, and neither is the reformulation of \Cref{sec:descriptor}. The paper's expected cost $O(1/\Delta)\,\mathrm{polylog}(1/\varepsilon)$ holds if $\Delta$ is the gap of the normalised $\tilde H$, which is $\Delta/\alpha_H$ in the Hamiltonian's own units; the cost is then $O(\alpha_H/\Delta)$, and the norm re-enters through the relative gap. The paper does not say which units it means. A second, smaller weakness sits on top of the first: the published prefactor $\alpha_{\G}^{\mathrm{pub}}$ exceeds $\|\G\|_2$ by a factor growing as $N^3$, so the published encoding is loose even against the matrix it encodes. The first weakness holds for any encoding and the second only for the published circuit; \Cref{sec:standard} separates them, and \Cref{sec:results-scaling} measures both.

\paragraph{Weaknesses in the evidence.} The paper's panels support the method less than they appear to. The printed constraint points are rounded and the inhomogeneous panels use Maclaurin-truncated sources, and each choice sets an error floor at the printed $n$ that no increase in resolution removes. The partial-differential panels read their extra invariant constraints off the known solution, which a new problem does not supply, and those constraints become degenerate beyond the printed $n$. The heat panel imposes its data at $t=0$, the end of its time domain, which makes it a backward heat problem and therefore ill posed. The filter is specified less completely than the Hamiltonian. Its time rule \Cref{eq:pihm-time-rule}, which the paper calls approximate, needs the largest eigenvalue and the gap in advance, which the method does not supply, and when tested it falls short of the target accuracy; and the filtered state is the ground state only to the extent that the initial state overlaps it, with a success probability that falls with that overlap. Each of these is tested in \Cref{sec:results-reproduction}.

\paragraph{Weaknesses in scope.} The nonlinear route projects rather than prepares: the doubled space leaves a kernel of dimension at least $2^{2n-1}$, so a filter returns the projection of the initial state, and the construction is quadratic only (\Cref{sec:pihm-doubled,sec:results-nonlinear}). The readout identity treats the prepared state as exactly the ground state, although a filter followed by post-selection succeeds with probability below one, and it returns one point of the field per measurement campaign (\Cref{sec:results-reproduction,sec:pipeline-readout}).

\paragraph{The paper's open problems.} The paper's Discussion names two: ``studying the spectral gap dependence is an important question for the future work'', and the choice of basis, for which ``one guiding principle here can be the cost of differentiation, where the goal is minimizing the depth of quantum circuits to implement derivatives, multiplication etc.'' \cite{wu2025pihm}. Paine's concurrent work \cite{paine2026concurrent} compares alternative Hamiltonian constructions and function encodings and finds, on a few small instances, that the normalised gap depends on both, while stating that these results do not establish a general ordering. It gives neither the gap's scaling with resolution nor block-encoding circuits, so it raises the first problem without answering it. The first weakness above is the quantitative form of that problem: the gap must be set against the Hamiltonian's norm, and the norm is fixed by the differentiation matrix, which is the paper's second problem.

\paragraph{The questions this thesis answers.} Three questions follow, each answered by one chapter. \emph{(i) Is the cost of the method inherent to the Hamiltonian its construction defines, or incurred by the circuits that block-encode it?} \Cref{sec:standard} rebuilds the published encoding and builds an exact structured one beside it, so that the circuit's cost can be separated from the spectrum's. \emph{(ii) Can the residual be reformulated so that its Hamiltonian is banded and well conditioned without changing its solution, and what does that cost in register size and spectral gap?} \Cref{sec:descriptor} carries the derivatives as unknowns tied by banded recurrences, instead of eliminating them into the dense $\G$. \emph{(iii) Can the solution of a nonlinear equation be prepared as the unique ground state of a Hamiltonian, rather than selected by projection from a degenerate kernel?} \Cref{sec:nonlinear} sets the doubled space against a Carleman lift, which prepares for a restricted class of polynomial equations, and measures separately whether the lift converges. Underlying all three is one question: whether the norm of the Hamiltonian, and with it the filter's degree, can be lowered by changing what the residual carries, and at what price.
