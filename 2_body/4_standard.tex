This chapter answers the first question of \Cref{sec:pihm-assessment}: is the cost of the physics-informed effective Hamiltonian paid by the circuit that block-encodes it, or set by the Hamiltonian itself? The two are separable because a filter's degree depends on the encoding only through its subnormalisation, $d=\Theta\bigl(\sqrt{\alpha_H/\Delta}\,\log(1/\varepsilon)\bigr)$ (\Cref{eq:degree}), and every block encoding of $H$ has $\alpha_H\ge\|H\|$. We call the excess of $\alpha_H/\|H\|$ the \emph{encoder's cost} and $\|H\|$ itself the \emph{Hamiltonian's cost}: the first belongs to the circuit, the second to the operator. We first reconstruct the method of Wu et al.\ \cite{wu2025pihm} as printed and test its claims mathematically (\Cref{sec:standard-rebuilt,sec:standard-claims}), and circuit by circuit (\Cref{sec:standard-published}). Then, we codify our own ``Standard Form'' by rectifying the algorithmic weaknesses of the faithful reconstruction. Then we provide an exact unitary encoding of this standard form and show the remaining costs (\Cref{sec:standard-factorisation,sec:standard-hamiltonian}).

We reproduce the construction of \Cref{sec:pihm-model,sec:pihm-residual} faithfully, keeping the choices of Wu et al.\ \cite{wu2025pihm} wherever the paper specifies them: its basis and feature vector, lifts, constraint points, Maclaurin truncations of the sources, the invariant sets of its partial differential equations, and the doubled space of its nonlinear panels (\Cref{sec:pihm-doubled}). Two things differ from the print and are stated: $\G$ acts on coefficient vectors where the paper prints $\G_n^{\top}$ (\Cref{sec:pihm-residual}), and the filter is applied by \gls{gqsp} in place of the paper's \gls{qsvt} (below). One panel runs through the chapter to make the choices concrete: the paper's Fig.~3b, a critically damped oscillator $f''+4f'+4f=0$ with $f(-1)=0$ and $f(0)=\tfrac12$, whose solution is $f=\tfrac12(1+x)e^{-2x}$ and which the paper prints at $n=3$. We label it R2a in the benchmark set of \Cref{tab:ladder}; its data involve no rounded point and no truncated source, so the printed and exact variants of this panel coincide.

The scale of the model is the paper's $\eta$ (\Cref{sec:pihm-model}), and its ideal value, the squared norm $\|b\|^2$ of the solution's normalised coefficient vector, has a closed form. With $x=\cos\theta$ the Chebyshev weight turns $\int T_jT_k(1-x^2)^{-1/2}\,dx$ into $\int_0^\pi\cos j\theta\cos k\theta\,d\theta$, so the normalised basis is orthogonal with the common squared norm $\pi/N$, and Parseval's identity gives
\begin{equation}
    \eta_e = \|b\|^2 = \frac{N}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{f_N(x)^2}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\,dx ,
    \label{eq:eta-e}
\end{equation}
where $f_N=\braket{\tau(x)|b}$ is the solution's truncated Chebyshev series. It is the physical field $f_Q$ when $\eta=\eta_e$ and $\ket{\psi}=\ket{b}/\|b\|$. For a smooth solution $f_N\to f$, so $\eta_e/N\to\pi^{-1}\int f^2(1-x^2)^{-1/2}\,dx$, and for a polynomial of degree below $N$ the two agree exactly. The ideal value is thus fixed by the solution and the resolution alone. The paper's printed $\eta$ equals it only to the extent that its ground state approximates $b/\|b\|$, so agreement tests the basis convention, the resolution and the accuracy of the model together.

The classical reference is the smallest right singular vector of the stacked residual $R$ with $H=R^\dagger R$ (\Cref{sec:standard-passage}), not an eigenvector of $H$, since forming $H$ squares the condition number of $R$. Throughout \Cref{sec:standard-rebuilt,sec:standard-audit}, $H$ and $R$ are the paper's operators; the standard form's are $\Hstd$ and $R_{\mathrm{std}}$. The distinction matters at $n=8$, where the relative gap $\Delta/\|H\|$ of R2a is $\res[2,sci]{build/R2a/standard/n8}{relative_gap}$, below machine epsilon $\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}=2^{-52}$ (\Cref{app:eigh-vs-svd}). The error of an eigenvector computed by a symmetric eigensolver is bounded only by $\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}\|H\|/\Delta$ \cite{daviskahan1970rotation}, which here exceeds one. A backward-stable SVD returns the exact singular vectors of some $R+\delta R$ with $\|\delta R\|_2\lesssim\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}\,\sigma_{\max}$, up to a modest dimension-dependent constant, so by Wedin's $\sin\Theta$ theorem \cite{wedin1972perturbation} the computed vector makes with the exact one an angle
\begin{equation}
    \sin\vartheta \lesssim \frac{\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}\,\sigma_{\max}}{\sigma_1-\sigma_0},
    \label{eq:wedin}
\end{equation}
again up to a constant of order one. Here $\sigma_0<\sigma_1$ are the two smallest singular values of $R$ and $\sigma_{\max}$ its largest, and the bound requires $\sigma_0$ to be isolated, which is the trusted-kernel condition (\Cref{sec:pipeline-reference}). When $\sigma_0\ll\sigma_1$ the denominator is $\sigma_1\approx\sqrt{\Delta}$, so the bound is about $\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}\sqrt{\|H\|/\Delta}$: the square root of the eigensolver's. The SVD therefore resolves a gap that the eigensolver cannot. The reference is as accurate as the residual's own spectrum allows, and no more. An iterative reference takes over beyond the dense SVD's reach, and a result is judged only where its kernel is trusted.

The reproduction is tested on every example of the paper's Figs~3--7: eleven \glspl{ode} (Figs~3--5), three \glspl{pde} (Fig.~6) and two nonlinear \glspl{ode} in doubled space (Fig.~7), at the resolutions the paper uses. Each example's scale is compared against the printed $\eta$ and $\eta_e$, its decoded field against the analytic solution, and the state that the paper's imaginary-time filter prepares against the classical reference. The paper's imaginary-time filter is applied by a \gls{gqsp} circuit in place of the paper's \gls{qsvt}, which removes the parity split and the LCU that recombines it (\Cref{sec:bg-qsp,tab:deviations}); the polynomial is unchanged. It is run by exact emulation and by circuit simulation (\Cref{sec:pipeline-filter}).

The matrices that the paper prints in its supplementary material at $n=2$ are reproduced entry by entry (the worked instance is in \Cref{app:sm-matrices}), and the printed scale of each of the eleven \glspl{ode} to within one unit in its last printed digit. The printed scales of the five \gls{pde} and nonlinear \gls{ode} panels are not reproduced, neither by $\eta_e$ of the exact solution nor by any ground state we computed, and the cause is not settled: \Cref{sec:results-reproduction} gives the numbers and the hypotheses we tested.

Four statements about them do not carry beyond those cases. \paragraph{``Positive-definite with distinct eigenvalues''.} The paper describes $H$ as a real symmetric positive-definite matrix with distinct eigenvalues, and elsewhere as a positive semi-definite form whose ground state has zero energy \cite{wu2025pihm}. Only the second holds. As a sum of Gram matrices, $H=R^\dagger R$ is positive semi-definite, and it is definite exactly when no nonzero vector satisfies every row of $R$. The method relies on the opposite case: when the truncated basis represents the solution exactly, as a polynomial solution of degree below $N$ does, $H$ has an exact zero eigenvalue. Distinctness fails when the constraints under-determine the solution. The paper's Laplace invariant set leaves the bottom of the spectrum near-degenerate at $n=(4,4)$, where the gap of $H$ relative to its norm is $\res[2,sci]{build/R5a/faithful/standard/n4x4}{relative_gap}$, and degenerate beyond it (below). The statement is therefore loose wording for a semi-definite $H$ whose ground state is isolated at the printed resolutions. The thesis relies on that isolation, measured as the gap ratio of a trusted kernel (\Cref{sec:pipeline-reference}), and never on definiteness.

\paragraph{A unique solution from the printed constraints.} The paper selects the solution by invariant constraints, each setting a derivative at a point to zero, and fixes its scale by one regular datum \cite{wu2025pihm}. Take a homogeneous ordinary equation of order $k$ whose leading coefficient has no zero on $[-1,1]$. Its solutions form a $k$-dimensional space $S$, and those that satisfy the constraints form a subspace of dimension $k-\rho$, where $\rho$ is the rank of the constraints restricted to $S$. The solution is unique up to scale exactly when $\rho=k-1$: fewer leave a degenerate bottom of the spectrum, and $\rho=k$ leaves only $f=0$, which no nonzero datum can match. The rank is that of the constraints restricted to $S$, not of the functionals, so $k-1$ constraints chosen without regard to the equation can fail, and the selected solution must not vanish at the regular point. R2a meets the condition (the count is proved in \Cref{app:uniqueness-count}). R3a, whose leading coefficient vanishes at the endpoints, is outside the lemma and is judged by its computed kernel, which is trusted at the printed $n$ (\Cref{sec:results-reproduction}). The count concerns the continuous problem. Whether the truncated $H$ has a one-dimensional kernel and a usable gap at a given $n$ is a separate question, answered by computing it, and three of the paper's \gls{ode} panels (R2b, R3c and R3d) have no trusted ground state at the printed $n$ (\Cref{sec:results-reproduction}). With a source the equation is inhomogeneous, and uniqueness is likewise judged by the computed kernel.

For a \gls{pde} the count has no analogue: the solutions form an infinite-dimensional space, and the invariant sets the paper prints omit the initial or boundary function that fixes the solution. The panels run regardless, because the truncation decides what the set leaves open. At the printed $n$ the kernel of $R$ is one-dimensional and trusted on all three, and the Laplace, heat and wave panels reach field errors of \res[2,sci]{build/R5a/faithful/standard/n3x3}{field_err_linf}, \res[2,sci]{build/R5b/faithful/standard/n4x4}{field_err_linf} and \res[2,sci]{build/R5c/faithful/standard/n5x5}{field_err_linf}. The Laplace set shows what is left open. The modes $\sin\tfrac{\ell\pi(x+1)}{2}\,\sinh\tfrac{\ell\pi(y+1)}{2}$, $\ell\ge1$, all satisfy the equation and its three homogeneous sides, and only $\ell=1$ matches the top-side function, which reaches the model through the regular point alone. As $n$ grows the other modes become representable and the kernel grows, to dimension $\res{build/R5a/faithful/standard/n5x5}{kernel_dimension}$ at $n=(5,5)$; the heat and wave sets degenerate in the same way, at a larger $n$ than the printed one (\Cref{sec:results-reproduction}). The heat and wave sets also carry constraints read off the known solution (the zeros of $\partial_xf$ at $x=\pm\tfrac34$ and the zeros at $t=\tfrac18,\tfrac78$), which a new problem does not supply. The paper presents each panel at one $n$, so this is a limit of the printed sets and not an error in its panels: they select a unique solution at the printed $n$ and lose that property as the truncation grows. \Cref{sec:standard-passage} replaces the invariant sets with the data themselves, imposed on a slice.

\paragraph{The time rule.} The paper evolves $e^{-\beta H}$ on the block-encoded $\tilde H=H/\|H\|_F$ for a time bounded below by $\beta_{\mathrm{pub}}$ (\Cref{eq:pihm-time-rule}); it calls the bound approximate and reports that the times $t=15$ and $t=8$ ``are enough'' for two of its examples \cite{wu2025pihm}, but gives no argument that connects the rule to an accuracy: $\varepsilon$ appears nowhere in it. We supply that argument here. Write $\tilde\lambda$ for the eigenvalues of $\tilde H$, so that $0\le\tilde\lambda\le\|\tilde H\|_F=1$, and take $n\ge2$. After the evolution the first excited state keeps the fraction $e^{-\beta(\tilde\lambda_2-\tilde\lambda_{\min})}$ of the ground state's amplitude. At $\beta_{\mathrm{pub}}$ the exponent is $(\tilde\lambda_2-\tilde\lambda_{\min})\,\tilde\lambda_{\max}/\bigl(\tilde\lambda_2(n-1)\bigr)$, which is at most $1/(n-1)$ because $\tilde\lambda_2-\tilde\lambda_{\min}\le\tilde\lambda_2$ and $\tilde\lambda_{\max}\le1$. The excited state therefore keeps at least $e^{-1/(n-1)}$ of the ground state's amplitude, a bound that tends to one as $n$ grows. Suppression to a fraction $\varepsilon$ needs $\beta_\varepsilon=\ln(1/\varepsilon)/(\tilde\lambda_2-\tilde\lambda_{\min})$, and
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\beta_\varepsilon}{\beta_{\mathrm{pub}}}=\frac{(n-1)\ln(1/\varepsilon)\,\tilde\lambda_2}{(\tilde\lambda_2-\tilde\lambda_{\min})\,\tilde\lambda_{\max}}\ \ge\ (n-1)\ln\frac1\varepsilon ,
    \label{eq:time-rule-shortfall}
\end{equation}
so the shortest time the rule permits is shorter than the one that suppresses the excited spectrum to $\varepsilon$ by at least that factor. The rule is a lower bound, so a longer time is not excluded, but it does not say how much longer. At the rule's own time, what the state keeps of the excited spectrum is set by its initial overlap with the lowest excited states and not by $\varepsilon$. In exact emulation (tier T2), from the geometric product state, the filter-prepared state at the paper's time has infidelity $\res[2]{protocol/R2a/standard/n3}{paper_infidelity}$ on R2a at $n=3$, and \Cref{sec:results-reproduction} gives the other examples. The rule is therefore an estimate of the scale of the evolution time and not a guarantee of accuracy, which the paper does not claim for it.

\paragraph{The readout identity.} The paper's interferometric readout recovers the signed overlap $\braket{\tau(x)|\psi_g}$ from two all-zero probabilities, $P_G(x)$ for the prepared state and $P_C(x)$ for it superposed with a reference, and its Eq.~36 takes the preparation to deliver the ground state with certainty \cite{wu2025pihm}. A filter followed by post-selection delivers it with probability $p_G<1$: on the all-zero ancilla the system holds $\sqrt{p_G}\,\psi_g$, and the rest of the state, supported on other ancilla values, contributes nothing to the all-zero outcome. Carrying $p_G$ through the same algebra gives, on one axis, $4P_C-P_G-1/(2N)=\sqrt{p_G/N}\,\tau(x)\cdot\psi_g$ (\Cref{eq:readout-identity}), of which the paper's identity is the case $p_G=1$; the derivation is in \Cref{app:readout-identity}. The field is unchanged, because $p_G$ cancels in the ratio against the regular datum. The scale is not: applied as printed, the identity returns an overlap smaller by $\sqrt{p_G}$ and so an $\eta_e$ larger by $1/p_G$ (\Cref{eq:readout-field}). It is correct for the deterministic preparation the paper assumes and needs the correction otherwise. The protocol is built and checked by circuit simulation (tier T1) against exact emulation (T2) on R1, R2a and R5a at small $n$ only, and the rest of the thesis does not use it: the field is decoded classically from the prepared state (\Cref{sec:pipeline-readout}).

The claims above are tested by algebra and the assembled residual alone. The remaining claim, that the operators are efficient to implement, concerns circuits and is tested next, on the paper's own block encodings (\Cref{sec:standard-published}).

Wu et al.\ report that each term of their Hamiltonian block-encodes with polynomially many gates (\Cref{sec:pihm-encoding}). We rebuild the three circuits of their Figs~2, 8 and 9 \cite{wu2025pihm} and test each claim against the rebuilt circuit. The registers, the angles and every deviation from the printed figures are given in \Cref{app:published-build}. The point-evaluation row $\Brow(x)$ and the collapse row $\Dzero(x_s)$ (Figs~2 and 8) rebuild exactly, realising the printed operator with our own wiring: $n+2$ qubits at subnormalisation $\sqrt{2^{n+1}}$, divided by $|f(x_s)|$ for $\Dzero$, whose sign the circuit carries as a phase. Their post-selection succeeds with probability $\|\tau(x)\|^2/2$, which averages one half over the Chebyshev measure, so the paper's ``high probability'' is a constant, not near-certainty.

We do not rebuild the derivative circuit (Fig.~9) wire for wire. The figure draws one instance, $n=3$, and leaves the control pattern of its rotations to the caption's bit string, so the general-$n$ wiring would be our inference rather than the paper's specification. We rebuild its mechanism with registers of our own instead. A uniform superposition over offsets $d$ and a register subtraction select the entry $(k,p)$ with $p-k=d$, and ladders of small controlled rotations load the column value $2p$ as an amplitude and correct row~0 by its factor $1/\sqrt2$. The superposition runs over odd offsets only, since a superposition over every offset would also load values onto the diagonal and onto the other entries of $\G$ that vanish. The rotation ladder composes angles exactly, but $\sin\bigl(\sum_m\arcsin(2^m\mu)\bigr)\neq\mu p$, so the loaded entries are approximate. To leading order the block differs from $\G$ entrywise by at most $\tfrac13\mu^2p^3$ (\Cref{prop:epsilon-G}), which at the printed $\mu = 2/\|\G\|_S$ is $O(N^{-5})$ and falls by a factor approaching $32$ per added qubit, consistent with the paper's exponential decrease.

\Cref{tab:published-verdicts} gives the verdict on each claim. The gate count holds as a bound, the error holds to leading order in $\mu$, the rows of Figs~2 and 8 hold exactly, and the qubit count of Fig.~9 is untested: our rebuild takes $2n+5$ qubits against the printed $2n+3$, a difference that neither confirms nor refutes the paper's count. The claim that decides the cost is the prefactor, which is valid but loose. A circuit with polynomially many gates is cheap to apply, but the number of times the filter must apply it is set by its subnormalisation (\Cref{eq:degree}), and the printed prefactor \Cref{eq:pihm-published-prefactor} exceeds $\|\G\|_2$ by a factor of $\res[2]{derivative/n7}{published_ratio}$ at $n=7$. The factor tends to $jN^3$, where $j\approx1.06$ is the first positive zero of the Bessel function $J_{-3/4}$ (\Cref{app:alpha-G}). This is the encoder's cost of the published circuit: its terms are efficient in gates and inefficient in subnormalisation. Our rebuild's own $\alpha$ is a third of the printed prefactor ($N\|\G\|_S/2$ against $3N\|\G\|_S/2$), so every published-encoding cost in this thesis is the kinder reading of the paper's. Because the encoding is approximate, no state is prepared with it; it enters only the resource estimates, composed into the same stacked residual as the structured encoding so that its filter degree is counted in the same way (\Cref{sec:standard-hamiltonian,sec:results-scaling}).

\subsubsection{The Factorisation}

% ~1 pp
% LAND, in order:
%   1. The idea in words FIRST: every entry of G is (a row-0 weight) x (a
%      parity-selected shift) x (the column index).
%   2. prop:G-factorisation (claim C-01). The proposition is exact; quote the
%      measured error as VERIFICATION, not as evidence.
% OFFLOAD: proof -> Appendix E (app:proof-G-factorisation).

The published circuit is approximate, and its prefactor large, because it loads the column value of each entry as a rotation amplitude. The structure of $\G$ allows a different route. Every non-zero entry of \Cref{eq:pihm-G-entries} is a product of three factors, each depending on one index alone: a row weight, equal to $1/\sqrt2$ in row~0 because of the normalisation of $T_0$ and to one elsewhere; a selector depending only on the offset $p-k$, non-zero when $p>k$ and $p-k$ is odd; and the column value $2p$, the weight with which differentiation passes $T_p$ to the lower polynomials. Each factor has an exact block encoding with no amplitude loaded by rotation.

\begin{proposition}[Exact factorisation of the differentiation matrix]
\label{prop:G-factorisation}
% STATE: for every n >= 1 the normalised-basis differentiation matrix
%   factorises exactly as below, with R_0 a row-0 reweighting, U_odd the
%   sum of the N/2 truncated shifts by odd offsets and Lambda the column index.
For every $n \ge 1$ the differentiation matrix on the normalised Chebyshev coefficients factorises as
\begin{equation}
    \begin{gathered}
        \G = R_0\,\bigl(2\Uodd\bigr)\,\Lambda, \\
        R_0 = I - \Bigl(1 - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\Bigr)\ket{0}\bra{0},
        \qquad
        \bigl(\Uodd\bigr)_{kp} = \bigl[\,p > k,\ p - k\ \text{odd}\,\bigr],
        \qquad
        \Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(p) ,
    \end{gathered}
    \label{eq:G-structured}
\end{equation}
where $\Uodd$ is the sum of the $N/2$ truncated shifts by odd offsets.
\end{proposition}

The proof compares the two sides entry by entry with \Cref{eq:pihm-G-entries} and is given in \Cref{app:proof-G-factorisation}. As a check on our implementation, not on the proposition, the block of the composed circuit matches the independently constructed $\G$ entrywise to $\prov{3.4\times10^{-13}}$ (absolute) for $n\le7$. The factorisation also fixes the size of $\G$: the subnormalisation $N(N-1)$ of \Cref{prop:standard-cost} bounds $\|\G\|_2$ above by $O(N^2)$, and the matching limit $\|\G\|_2/N^2\to 1/(2j)$, with the $j$ of \Cref{sec:standard-published}, is proved in \Cref{app:proof-standard-cost}.

\subsubsection{Circuits and Cost}
\label{sec:standard-cost}
% ~1 pp
% LAND, in order:
%   1. Lambda as a Z-string LCU (eq:lambda-lcu): alpha = N - 1,
%      ceil(log2(n+1)) selector qubits, O(n) gates.
%   2. U_odd: a uniform PREPARE over odd offsets; SELECT as the in-place subtraction
%      p <- p - d with a post-selected borrow flag, so wrapped terms vanish
%      EXACTLY (alpha = N/2, O(n) Toffolis). This is the one representative
%      circuit (F3).
%   3. R_0: a two-term LCU of I and I - 2|0><0|, alpha = 1.
%   4. prop:standard-cost. The Omega(n) ancilla floor comes from the N/2-term
%      LCU -- a genuine regime difference from the descriptor's O(log n).
%   5. Lifts M_{x^p}; rank-one rows via the feature map; products with ancilla reuse.
% OFFLOAD: the arithmetic SELECT and the resource derivation -> Appendix E
%   (app:structured).
% FIG: fig:uodd-select (F3). TAB: tab:alpha-G-short; the full table is
%   tab:alpha-G-full in Appendix E.

Each factor is encoded as a linear combination of unitaries \cite{childs2012lcu} whose terms are unitary already, so no part of the encoding is approximate. The column index is a sum of Pauli strings,
\begin{equation}
    \Lambda = \frac{N-1}{2}\,I - \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} 2^{j-1} Z_j ,
    \label{eq:lambda-lcu}
\end{equation}
since $p=\sum_j2^j(1-z_j)/2$ in terms of the eigenvalues $z_j$ of $Z_j$. Its $n+1$ terms need $\lceil\log_2(n+1)\rceil$ selector qubits and $O(n)$ gates, and its subnormalisation $\alpha_\Lambda=N-1$ equals $\|\Lambda\|_2$. The row weight is the two-term combination $R_0=aI+b\,(I-2\ket{0}\!\bra{0})$ with $a=\tfrac12(1+1/\sqrt2)$ and $b=\tfrac12(1-1/\sqrt2)$, both positive, so $\alpha_{R_0}=1=\|R_0\|_2$.

The sum of shifts is the one factor that needs arithmetic (\Cref{fig:uodd-select}). Its PREPARE puts an offset register in the uniform superposition over the $N/2$ odd offsets, by an $X$ on the lowest qubit and Hadamards on the rest. Its SELECT is the in-place subtraction $p\leftarrow p-d$ of a ripple-carry circuit \cite{cuccaro2004adder}, which sets a borrow flag when $p<d$. The terms that would wrap around carry a borrow of one, and post-selecting the flag on zero removes them exactly. The block is therefore $\tfrac{2}{N}\bigl[p>k,\ p-k\ \text{odd}\bigr]$, an exact encoding of $\Uodd$ at $\alpha=N/2$ with $O(n)$ Toffoli gates, and one PREPARE serves all $N/2$ terms because SELECT is a permutation (\Cref{app:uodd-select-detail}).

The three encodings multiply, and so do their subnormalisations. Their product is an exact block encoding of $\G$ at $\alpha_{\G}=1\cdot N\cdot(N-1)$, with the following cost.

\begin{proposition}[Cost of the structured encoding]
\label{prop:standard-cost}
% STATE: the factorisation of prop:G-factorisation compiles to an exact block
%   encoding of G with the subnormalisation, gate count and ancilla count below.
The factorisation of \Cref{prop:G-factorisation} compiles to an exact block encoding of $\G$ on $n$ system qubits with
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_{\G} = N(N-1),
    \qquad
    \frac{\alpha_{\G}}{\|\G\|_2} \longrightarrow 2j_{-3/4,1} \approx 2.117,
    \qquad
    O(n^2)\ \text{gates},
    \qquad
    n + 4\ \text{ancilla} ,
    \label{eq:standard-cost}
\end{equation}
of which $n-1$ ancilla are the selector of the $N/2$-term combination $\Uodd$.
\end{proposition}
The limit is that of $\|\G\|_2/N^2$ above; the gate count assumes an ancilla-free synthesis of multi-controlled gates, and falls to $O(n\log n)$ with a linear-cost synthesis that borrows idle qubits \cite{barenco1995elementary}. The proof is given in \Cref{app:proof-standard-cost}. The ancilla count is exact in the circuit for $n=2$ to $8$. The transpiled two-qubit gate counts are available only at small $n$ (\prov{109} at $n=2$ and \prov{471} at $n=5$), consistent with the bound but too few to fit it. The limit is asymptotic, and the sizes we reach are measured: the ratio $\alpha_{\G}/\|\G\|_2$ is $\res[3]{derivative/n2}{structured_ratio}$ at $n=2$ and rises to $\res[3]{derivative/n10}{structured_ratio}$ at $n=10$ (\Cref{tab:alpha-G-short}), against a published ratio that grows as $N^3$. The structured encoding is this close to the norm because $\alpha_{R_0}$ and $\alpha_\Lambda$ equal their factors' norms. Its looseness comes from $\Uodd$, whose $\alpha$ exceeds its norm by at most $\pi/2$, and from the product, in which the large entries of $\Lambda$ meet the columns of $\Uodd$ with few non-zero entries.

The $n-1$ selector qubits are not an artefact of this circuit. A combination of $L$ terms needs $\lceil\log_2L\rceil$ selector qubits, and lower bounds of this kind hold for exact block encodings built from $L$-term decompositions \cite{chakraborty2025qsvt}, so the count is the price of encoding the $N/2$ shifts of $\Uodd$ as one combination. It marks a structural difference between the two forms: the descriptor form of \Cref{sec:descriptor-block-encoding} never forms the sum of shifts, and its ancilla grow as $\log n$.

The remaining terms of the residual are built from the same pieces. A coefficient $a(x)$ multiplies a series through the exact lift $\M_a$ to $n+1$ qubits, which holds the product without truncation whenever $\deg a\le N$ (\Cref{app:lifts-folds}); it is encoded as a sum of powers of the single-$x$ lift, itself a combination of truncated shifts built by the same post-selected subtraction, so its subnormalisation is the sum of the absolute monomial coefficients weighted by powers of that lift's. The rank-one rows $\Brow(x)$ and $\Dzero(x_s)$ are the exact circuits of \Cref{sec:standard-published}, and a source given by its Chebyshev coefficients is loaded by one rotation tree. A power $\G^k$ is a product of encodings that share one ancilla register, with a failure flag per product, so $\G^2$ costs one qubit more than $\G$. The subnormalisation of a product is the product of the subnormalisations, so a power compounds the constant above while $\|\G^k\|_2<\|\G\|_2^k$. The stacked residual is then assembled into a single reflection (\Cref{sec:pipeline-walk}), and the composed circuit is verified against the independently assembled Hamiltonian (\Cref{sec:pipeline-verification}).

For constant coefficients the structured encoding removes the encoder's cost from the whole residual, not only from $\G$. We take as the running example the paper's second-order equation $f''+4f'+4f=0$, with the invariant constraint $f(-1)=0$ and the regular datum $f(0)=\tfrac12$ (its Fig.~3b, which we label R2a in the benchmark set of \Cref{tab:ladder}). At $n=8$ the structured encoding of its stacked residual has $\alpha_R=\res[3,sci]{stateprep/R2a/standard/n8/T3/filter=minimax}{alpha_R}$ against $\|R\|_2=\res[3,sci]{stateprep/R2a/standard/n8/T3/filter=minimax}{norm_R}$, a ratio of $\res[3]{looseness/stateprep/R2a/standard/n8/T3/filter=minimax}{alpha_ratio}$ (a resource estimate; the circuit at this size is not simulated). The ratio rises from $\res[3]{looseness/stateprep/R2a/standard/n2/T3/filter=minimax}{alpha_ratio}$ at $n=2$ with increments that roughly halve at each added qubit, so it is levelling off over $n=2$ to $8$, although we have not proved a limit for the whole residual (\Cref{sec:results-scaling}). The Hamiltonian is encoded at $\alpha_H=\alpha_R^2$, so $\alpha_H/\|\Hstd\|$ is the square of that ratio, from $\res[3]{looseness/stateprep/R2a/standard/n2/T3/filter=minimax}{hamiltonian_ratio}$ to $\res[3]{looseness/stateprep/R2a/standard/n8/T3/filter=minimax}{hamiltonian_ratio}$, whereas the published encoding's ratio for $\G$ alone grows as $N^3$.

No encoder can change the norm it encodes. For an equation of order $k$ with constant coefficients, a non-zero leading coefficient and constraints of lower order, $\|\G^k\|_2/N^{2k}$ tends to a positive constant, so $\|R_{\mathrm{std}}\|_2=\Theta(N^{2k})$ and $\|\Hstd\|=\|R_{\mathrm{std}}\|_2^2=\Theta(N^{4k})$; the proof is given in \Cref{app:proof-standard-cost}. The factor $N^2$ per derivative is the norm of $\G$ (\Cref{sec:standard-factorisation}), and the exponent doubles because the Hamiltonian is the Gram product $R^\dagger R$. The gap, by contrast, is measured, not proved. On the two one-axis problems with a non-vanishing leading coefficient checked so far, R2a and $f''-2f'-3f=0$ (R2b, the paper's Fig.~3c), it does not grow with $N$: on R2a $\Delta=\res[3]{build/R2a/standard/n7}{gap}$ at $n=7$ and $\res[3]{build/R2a/standard/n8}{gap}$ at $n=8$, while $\|\Hstd\|$ grows from $\res[2,sci]{build/R2a/standard/n7}{hamiltonian_norm}$ to $\res[2,sci]{build/R2a/standard/n8}{hamiltonian_norm}$. Since $\alpha_H\ge\|\Hstd\|$ for every block encoding, \Cref{eq:degree} gives
\begin{equation}
    \|\Hstd\| = \Theta\bigl(N^{4k}\bigr),
    \qquad
    \Delta = \Theta(1)\ \text{(measured)}
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    d = \tilde\Theta\bigl(N^{2k}\bigr),
    \label{eq:standard-wall}
\end{equation}
which at second order, the order of the paper's panels, is $\|\Hstd\|=\Theta(N^8)$ and $d=\tilde\Theta(N^4)$ whatever the block encoding. This is the Hamiltonian's cost. The floor is stated for the minimax filter that prepares every state in this thesis; for polynomial filters in general it is what the optimality of Lin and Tong's construction supports \cite{lintong2020groundstate}, and we claim no more. At $n=8$ the estimated degree for R2a is $\res[2,sci]{stateprep/R2a/standard/n8/T3/filter=minimax}{degree}$ as built and $\res[2,sci]{stateprep/R2a/standard/n8/T3/filter=minimax}{ideal_degree}$ at the ideal-encoding bound $\alpha=\|R\|_2$, both resource estimates far beyond what exact emulation reaches. The degree is linear in $\alpha_R$, so the ratio of the two is the encoder's looseness and the second is the Hamiltonian's share.

The polynomial gate count that Wu et al.\ report for their operators, $\G$, $\Brow$ and $\Dzero$ \cite{wu2025pihm}, and that our rebuild confirms (\Cref{sec:standard-published}), is the cost of one query to the block encoding. The cost of the method is that cost times the number of queries, and \Cref{eq:standard-wall} fixes the number of queries by the subnormalisation, however few gates a query takes. The paper does not claim otherwise: it leaves the gap, and so the preparation time, to future work \cite{wu2025pihm}. The claim is therefore true of the circuit and silent on the method, and polynomial gates per query do not make the standard form efficient, since its filter makes $\tilde\Theta(N^{2k})$ queries or more, which is exponential in $n$.

The growth of the degree between $n=7$ and $n=8$, again from resource estimates, shows the two costs apart. With the published encoding the degree grows as $N^{\res[3]{stateprep/R2a/standard/growth/T3/filter=minimax/encoder=published}{degree}}$, with the structured encoding as $N^{\res[3]{stateprep/R2a/standard/growth/T3/filter=minimax}{degree}}$, and at the ideal bound, which contains no encoding at all, as $N^{\res[3]{stateprep/R2a/standard/growth/T3/filter=minimax}{ideal_degree}}$. The first difference is the encoder's cost, which the structured encoding removes; the common exponent is the Hamiltonian's. It belongs to the least-squares functional, not to the circuits: $\Hstd=R^\dagger R$ is the normal-equations form of the least-squares problem for an operator of order $k$, and such a problem squares the conditioning of the operator it minimises. The classical remedies are to recast the equation as a first-order system \cite{cai1994fosls} or to use a basis in which differentiation is banded (\Cref{sec:bg-fosls,sec:bg-ultraspherical}).

The conclusion has a limited scope. The structured encoding bounds $\alpha_H/\|\Hstd\|$ for constant coefficients only, and is loose for a variable coefficient that is lifted. On the Legendre equation $(1-x^2)f''-2xf'+20f=0$ (R3a, the paper's Fig.~4a), the circuit pays for the product $\M_a\G^2$ as $\|\M_a\|\,\|\G^2\|\gg\|\M_a\G^2\|$, because the coefficient $1-x^2$ vanishes at the ends of the interval, where the derivatives of Chebyshev polynomials are largest. The measured norm of this residual grows as $N^2$, not as $N^{2k}=N^4$, and the encoder's cost reappears: the as-built degree grows as $N^{\res[3]{stateprep/R3a/standard/growth/T3/filter=minimax}{degree}}$, while at the ideal bound it grows as $N^{\res[3]{stateprep/R3a/standard/growth/T3/filter=minimax}{ideal_degree}}$, the exponent the descriptor's degree also has there ($N^{\res[3]{stateprep/R3a/descriptor/growth/T3/filter=minimax}{ideal_degree}}$ at the ideal bound). At $n=8$ the as-built degree is $\res[2,sci]{stateprep/R3a/standard/n8/T3/filter=minimax}{degree}$ against $\res[2,sci]{stateprep/R3a/standard/n8/T3/filter=minimax}{ideal_degree}$ at the ideal bound, both resource estimates. For this reason \Cref{sec:results-scaling} quotes the ideal-encoding bound beside every as-built cost, and where the comparison between the forms reverses at that bound, as it does on R3, reports the reversal as a finding.

This answers the first question. For the constant-coefficient problems of the paper's regime the cost of the published method has two parts: the encoder's, a factor growing as $N^3$ in the subnormalisation, which the structured encoding removes; and the Hamiltonian's, $\tilde\Theta(N^{2k})$ in the filter's degree, which no encoding removes. \Cref{sec:results-scaling} tests both on the benchmark problems. What remains open to change is the Hamiltonian itself, through what its residual carries, and \Cref{sec:descriptor} changes it by carrying the derivatives as unknowns instead of eliminating them into the dense $\G$.
