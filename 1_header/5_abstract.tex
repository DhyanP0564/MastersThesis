The cost of solving a differential equation numerically grows with the resolution it demands. A register of $n$ qubits holds $N=2^n$ amplitudes, so a quantum algorithm can store a discretised solution in exponentially few qubits. What such an algorithm costs is the number of times it must apply a circuit encoding the problem, and that number is set by how well conditioned the encoded problem is. Wu et al.\ \cite{wu2025pihm} recently proposed writing a differential equation as the ground state of a physics-informed effective Hamiltonian and preparing that state with a spectral filter in place of a variational search. Every term of their Hamiltonian has an efficient circuit, but how many applications a preparation needs, a number that grows with the square root of the Hamiltonian's norm over its spectral gap, was left open.

This thesis locates the cost of that approach and reduces it. Rebuilding the method and reproducing its examples for ordinary differential equations, we separate the cost of encoding the Hamiltonian from that of the Hamiltonian itself. For a critically damped oscillator on eight qubits, an exact encoding of the differentiation matrix lowers the estimated number of applications from $\res[2,sci]{stateprep/R2a/standard/n8/T3/filter=minimax/encoder=published}{degree}$ with the published encoding to $\res[2,sci]{stateprep/R2a/standard/n8/T3/filter=minimax}{degree}$, and no encoding of the same Hamiltonian can bring it below $\res[2,sci]{stateprep/R2a/standard/n8/T3/filter=minimax}{ideal_degree}$. What remains is intrinsic to the Hamiltonian of this standard form, whose state holds the solution alone: each derivative is eliminated through the dense Chebyshev differentiation matrix, whose norm grows as $N^2$, so for a second-order equation with constant coefficients the Hamiltonian's norm grows as $N^8$ and the number of applications as $N^4$, however it is encoded.

We then propose a descriptor form of the Hamiltonian, which has the same ground state and carries the solution's derivatives as additional unknowns linked by sparse, banded relations. The idea adapts two classical methods, the ultraspherical spectral method and first-order-system least squares. Its circuit needs only $\mathcal{O}(\log n)$ auxiliary qubits. For linear problems on one axis with polynomial coefficients, analytic data, a leading coefficient that never vanishes and a solution unique up to scale, its norm grows only as $N^2$ and we prove that the spectral gap stays bounded away from zero as the resolution grows, so the number of applications grows about linearly in $N$; for the oscillator the estimate falls to $\res{stateprep/R2a/descriptor/n8/T3/filter=minimax}{degree}$, or $\res{stateprep/R2a/descriptor/n8/T3/filter=minimax}{ideal_degree}$ under an ideal encoding, although on constant-coefficient problems each application can cost more gates. The carried derivatives erode the gap wherever the equation fails to control them. On one axis, scaling each derivative block by a single weight restores it without extra qubits, and the number of applications then grows about as $N^2$ when the leading coefficient has a simple zero. On two axes the gap still closes, and the standard form can need fewer applications on Legendre's equation under an ideal encoding and on the heat equation at small sizes.

Both formulations extend to coupled systems and, through Carleman linearisation, to nonlinear equations. We show that the doubled-space construction proposed for nonlinear terms has a degenerate ground space, onto which a filter can only project, whereas the Carleman system has a unique ground state, which we prepare in both formulations for Burgers' equation by exact emulation and for two-dimensional incompressible Navier--Stokes flow on up to $48$ Fourier modes by exact emulation and resource estimate. A well-isolated ground state does not certify the nonlinear solution, however: at strong nonlinearity the linearisation diverges while its ground state stays unique.

All results are classical computations: the field is decoded from the prepared state's amplitudes, and nothing was run on quantum hardware. Circuits of up to $24$ qubits are verified against the operators they encode, and simulations of up to $\res{stateprep/R4d/descriptor/n3/T1/filter=minimax}{n_qubits}$ qubits with the minimax filter agree with exact emulation to within $10^{-10}$. Neither formulation offers an exponential speed-up, and no advantage over classical solvers is claimed. The broader lesson is that once the encoding is tight, a ground-state solver's cost is set by the operator being prepared, so reformulating the problem can matter more than optimising its circuit. The descriptor form moves the conditioning of differentiation from the norm into the gap, which can be secured on one axis but not yet on two.
