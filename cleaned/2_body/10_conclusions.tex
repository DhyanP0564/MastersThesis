Wu et al.\ priced their physics-informed effective Hamiltonian by the gates in one query, each polynomial in the number of qubits, and left open how its cost depends on the spectral gap and on the cost of differentiation \cite{wu2025pihm}. The number of queries is the degree of the filter that prepares the ground state, which grows as the square root of the encoding's subnormalisation over the gap, and two costs that a gate count cannot register are folded into it. The first is the encoder's, the excess of the subnormalisation over the Hamiltonian's norm. It is the larger factor in the degree that Wu et al.'s own circuits imply, and an exact structured encoding of the same residual removes it. The second is the Hamiltonian's, which no encoding reaches. In the standard form, whose state holds the field alone and eliminates every derivative through the dense Chebyshev differentiation matrix $\G$, the norm grows as $N^{4k}$ for an order-$k$ equation with constant coefficients, and the degree as $\tilde\Theta(N^{2k})$ under every block encoding of that residual and every polynomial filter of its walk. Once the encoder's excess is removed, the query count of the published method is set by the Hamiltonian it constructs, and the cost of differentiation that Wu et al.\ measured in circuit depth returns in the number of queries, as a factor of order $N^2$ in the degree for every derivative eliminated through $\G$.

That factor belongs to the way the residual is written, and it can be removed without changing the solution. Chebyshev differentiation is dense only as a product of two banded matrices, which are, up to a factor of two, the conversion and differentiation operators of the ultraspherical spectral method. The descriptor form keeps the two factors apart, carrying the intermediate derivatives of a higher-order equation as unknowns tied by banded recurrences and multiplying a first-order equation or system through by the conversion factor. Its kernel, and with it the prepared solution, is the standard form's, but the inverse that contributed a power of $N$ for each derivative is gone, together with the composition that multiplied those powers. The descriptor Hamiltonian's norm grows as $N^2$ at fixed block weights whatever the order, and on a regular problem, one axis with polynomial coefficients, a leading coefficient without zeros on $[-1,1]$ and analytic data, its gap is bounded below independently of $N$, so the degree grows as $\tilde\Theta(N)$. What leaves the norm reappears in the gap. The descriptor measures a state's residual against the derivatives it carries, so its gap stays bounded only where the equation controls every one of them. Near a simple zero of the leading coefficient that control is lost, and rescaling each block by one weight restores a gap of order one on a single axis, without ancilla and at a cost in gates fixed by the equation, leaving a degree of about $\tilde\Theta(N^2)$. On two axes the equation constrains only a sum of top derivatives, and the gap falls with $N$ whatever the weights. The remaining prices are constants: a register of derivative blocks, and more two-qubit gates per query than the structured standard form on constant-coefficient problems at the sizes built, so that the descriptor's advantage lies wholly in the degree. The choice of filter is likewise a constant. The minimax edge filter reaches a given suppression of the excited spectrum at a lower degree than Wu et al.'s imaginary-time filter, by a factor below two that does not drift with the resolution, and leaves the exponent where the residual put it.

Nonlinear equations pose the same question at its limit, where a degenerate ground space leaves no gap and the degree is undefined. Wu et al.'s doubled space encodes a polynomial nonlinearity exactly, but its zero-energy eigenspace has dimension at least $2^{2n-1}$, and a filter can only project the initial state onto it, in a direction the initial state chooses. A Carleman lift exchanges that exactness for uniqueness. The truncated lift is a coupled linear system with an isolated ground state, which both forms prepare, and this thesis carries it from Burgers' equation to two-dimensional incompressible Navier--Stokes in exact emulation and resource estimate. The reformulation's advantage survives the lift through its time axis, the one axis on which the lifted system still differentiates, where the descriptor's mass form lowers the degree once the time resolution is fine enough for the derivative to outweigh the coupling. The uniqueness that makes preparation possible certifies the truncated problem and nothing more: a lift past its boundary keeps a trusted, isolated ground state while its decoded field departs from the nonlinear solution, and nothing in the residual's spectrum signals the departure. Neither form, on a linear equation or a lifted one, is polylogarithmic in $N$. The three answers therefore describe a single mechanism. Ground-state preparation of a differential equation costs what the conditioning of its residual costs, and that conditioning is fixed when the residual is formulated, before any circuit is built.

The account reaches beyond this method. A gate count per query is accurate and incomplete, because the degree that multiplies it depends on the subnormalisation and on the conditioning, and a gate count sees neither. Separating the encoder's cost from the Hamiltonian's is an audit that any filter-based algorithm on a block-encoded operator admits, and a comparison of formulations needs both halves of it. Built circuits favour whichever form has the tighter encoding, which here is the descriptor, so only at the ideal-encoding bound $\alpha_R=\|R\|_2$ does a comparison speak about the Hamiltonians themselves, and where the order reverses at that bound the reversal is itself a finding. The same separation underwrote the evidence. A statevector simulation doubles its memory with every qubit, and what carried the study to the resolutions where scaling is visible was an exact emulation proved equal to the circuit's post-selected output and checked against the circuit wherever both run. For a classical study of any filter-based algorithm this is the practical route past the statevector ceiling, at a fraction of its cost.

The degree has the form of the optimal law for linear systems, the condition number times $\log(1/\varepsilon)$, with the residual's effective condition number in place of the matrix's. The ground-state route, which needs no linear solve and no training, therefore meets conditioning in another guise, as every quantum route to a differential equation pays a ratio that its discretisation fixes. Read this way, the standard form's degree is the conditioning of an order-$k$ least-squares discretisation and the descriptor's on a regular problem is that of a first-order one, the reduction of order that classical solvers and quantum linear-system algorithms for ordinary equations already make before they discretise. The tools for lowering that ratio are consequently those of classical numerical analysis. The descriptor transferred two of them, the banded operators of the ultraspherical method \cite{olver2013} and the carried derivatives of first-order-system least squares \cite{cai1994fosls}, and the rest of that literature, preconditioning and the weighted norms that make least-squares functionals coercive among it, bears as directly on a quantum filter's degree as on a classical solver's condition number. The conditioning is visible, moreover, before any quantum computer is involved: the standard form's classical least-squares solution loses accuracy at high resolution as its norm amplifies rounding, so the classical solve already diagnoses the quantum degree.

Against classical solvers the gain is narrower than a speed-up, and none is claimed. For an analytic solution the resolution needed for an accuracy $\delta$ grows only as $\log(1/\delta)$, so a comparison of exponents in $N$ implies no advantage in precision. On one axis a degree linear in $N$ matches the operation count of the ultraspherical method, which solves such an equation in $\mathcal O(N)$ operations once its coefficients are resolved, and decoding the whole field from a quantum state needs of order $N$ estimates in any case. An advantage could appear only on several axes, where a classical solver's cost grows at least as the number of unknowns, $N^D$ on $D$ axes, and that is precisely where the descriptor's gap is least secure. What the reformulation does make possible is a preparation whose cost tracks the classical one in order on a regular problem, where the standard form's exceeds it by a factor $N^{2k-1}$, and the preparation of nonlinear flows as unique ground states. The residual also serves as an interface. Any condition that annihilates the solution enters as rows, so coupled fields, complex-valued linear equations after realification and, in principle, observed data reach the same filter and the same verification without change to either. For Carleman methods in general the lifts studied here carry a further lesson. The condition $\rho<1$ under which Liu et al.\ prove that the truncation error falls geometrically is a worst case over all data of a given norm \cite{liu2021carleman}; on structured data it is far from necessary, and convergence follows the ratio $a/\nu$ of the data's amplitude to the viscosity instead. A lift can also keep an isolated ground state after it has stopped converging, so any method that relies on a truncated lift's spectrum certifies the truncation and needs a separate test of the physics.

Two boundaries limit how far these conclusions extend, and each has a definite cause. The first is the gap, to which the reformulation moved the conditioning. Its flatness on a regular problem is a discrete coercivity, the property on which first-order-system least squares also rests, and its collapse on two axes reads as the failure of that coercivity, an inference from the mechanism that the measurements are consistent with but do not establish. The boundary therefore lies where an advantage over classical solvers could exist, which makes it the central open problem of this route. The second boundary separates a prepared state from an answer. The spectral edge of each preparation here came from a classical solution and a lift's convergence was judged against a classical reference, while the field itself was decoded classically from amplitudes that a quantum computer exposes only through measurement. Where the method would matter, beyond classical reach, none of those references exists. The qubit counts stay modest, and the obstacle to hardware is depth: a filter's queries run in sequence, which places even the smallest preparation on an error-corrected machine, and no preparation here is a candidate for a noisy device.

\label{sec:future-work}
The most consequential open question is whether any residual keeps its gap on several axes, since its answer decides whether this route can compete with classical spectral solvers at all. First-order-system least squares meets the same loss of coercivity with boundary residuals measured in fractional-order norms, which suggests weighting the datum rows of Laplace's equation (R5a) by a diagonal that grows with the Chebyshev index. Such weights leave an exact kernel unchanged, so the gap and the norm they produce at three to five qubits per axis would test the idea directly. The other candidate is the full ultraspherical reduction, a banded residual on the field alone that removes the inverse in $\G$ without carrying any derivative; whether it keeps a norm of order $N^2$ without eroding the gap is unknown, and one problem of each class, R2a, Legendre's equation (R3) and the heat equation (R5b), would settle it. Beneath both lies a question about the floor. Block rescaling weights the kernel by one constant per block and so stays cheap, whereas an equilibration per amplitude would need a block encoding of its own at a cost linear in the dimension. Whether a diagonal structured in the Chebyshev index, cheap to encode where the general one is not, can do for the degree what diagonal preconditioning does for the ultraspherical method's banded systems \cite{olver2013}, or whether a degree linear in $N$ is the least that a differentiating residual reaches on one axis, remains open.

A second direction would take the classical reference out of the loop, since beyond classical reach a solver must certify its own output. An underestimated gap raises the degree only as the inverse square root of the bound, so a lower bound of the right order in $N$ would preserve every law established here. For constant coefficients such a bound could come from a quantitative form of the flat-gap theorem, replacing its compactness argument with the explicit solution operator of the equation and its constraints, and checked against the gaps already measured on the decay and second-order problems (R1, R2). Singular and two-axis problems have no theorem to sharpen, and their edge would have to be estimated on the circuit from the eigenphases of the qubitised walk, a harder task because an initial state chosen without the answer has no reason to weigh the edge. The lift admits a test that reads no reference: whether the fields decoded at consecutive Carleman orders separate convergence from divergence can be checked classically on the references already computed, which would also locate the boundary that Burgers' lifts (R9) bracket between $a/\nu=3$ and $10$ without resolving. The output needs the same treatment, since every cost here stops at the prepared state, and a shot-count study of Wu et al.'s interferometric readout on R2a would supply the factor those costs omit.

The last direction concerns what the residual carries. A complex residual still gives a Hermitian Gram operator, so native complex preparation needs complex loaders and no new filter, and $z'=-\mathrm{i}\pi z$, which R7 carries realified, is the natural first comparison of the two encodings. An observation $f(x_i)=y_i$ would enter as the row $y_sf(x_i)-y_if(x_s)=0$ against the regular datum $f(x_s)=y_s$, but noisy observations replace the exact kernel with a least-squares fit, and whether that fit keeps a gap a filter can use has not been studied; it marks the point at which a physics-informed Hamiltonian would become a data-informed one. Each of these changes the rows and leaves the filter and its verification as they are. The work that remains is therefore work on the formulation. Wu et al.\ built this route from its circuits outward, and the evidence of this thesis is that its cost is decided by the residual, which is best conditioned before it is encoded.
