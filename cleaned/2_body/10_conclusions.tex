\subsection{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions-answers}

Wu et al.\ priced their physics-informed effective Hamiltonian by the gates in one query and left open how its cost depends on the spectral gap and on the cost of differentiation \cite{wu2025pihm}. Question (i) of \Cref{sec:pihm-assessment} asked how the number of queries, the filter's degree, grows with the resolution, and how much of that growth is the encoder's and how much the Hamiltonian's. Wu et al.'s circuits indicate the larger factor is the encoder's, but some unreducable amount is the Hamiltonian's; checked by an exact encoding as well as ideal bounds. In the standard form, whose state holds the field alone and represents every derivative through the dense Chebyshev differentiation matrix $\G$, the degree grows as $\tilde\Theta(N^{2k})$ for an order-$k$ equation with constant coefficients under any block encoding and every polynomial filter of the walk.

Question (ii) asked whether the residual can be rewritten so that its Hamiltonian is banded and well conditioned without changing its solution, and at what cost. The descriptor form keeps apart the two banded factors whose product is $\G$, carrying a higher-order equation's derivatives as unknowns and multiplying a first-order equation or system through by the conversion factor. Its kernel is equivalent to the standard form's. On a regular problem with one axis, polynomial coefficients, a leading coefficient without zeros on $[-1,1]$ and analytic data, its degree grows as $\tilde\Theta(N)$ independent of order. The conditioning moves the norm's cost to the gap. On a singular problem whose leading coefficient has a simple zero, block rescaling restores a gap of order one on a single axis, without ancilla and at a cost in gates fixed by the equation, for a degree of about $\tilde\Theta(N^2)$. On two axes the gap falls with $N$ whatever the weights. The descriptor's other prices, a register of derivative blocks and, on constant-coefficient problems at the sizes built, more gates per query than the structured standard form, are factors independent of $N$, so its advantage lies wholly in the degree.

Nonlinear equations, the subject of question (iii), pose the same question at its limit. Wu et al.'s doubled space encodes a polynomial nonlinearity exactly, but its zero-energy eigenspace has dimension at least $2^{2n-1}$, so a filter can only project onto it. A truncated Carleman lift has an isolated ground state, which both forms prepare, from Burgers' equation to two-dimensional incompressible Navier--Stokes in exact emulation and resource estimate. The descriptor's advantage survives through the time axis --- which the lift still differentiates --- once that axis is resolved finely enough. The uniqueness that makes preparation possible certifies the truncated problem only; a lift past its boundary keeps an isolated ground state while its field departs from the nonlinear solution. The condition $\rho<1$ under which Liu et al.\ prove that the truncation converges is a worst case over all data, which structured data need not meet \cite{liu2021carleman}.

The three answers describe one mechanism: ground-state preparation of a differential equation costs what the conditioning of its residual costs, and the formulation fixes that conditioning before circuit encodings are created. Neither form, on a linear equation or a lifted one, is polylogarithmic in $N$. Separating the encoder's cost from the Hamiltonian's is an audit that any filter-based algorithm on a block-encoded operator admits; because built circuits favour the form with the tighter encoding, a comparison speaks about the Hamiltonians only at the ideal-encoding bound $\alpha_R=\|R\|_2$. The degree has the form of the optimal law for linear systems, with the residual's effective condition number in place of the matrix's, so the ground-state route meets conditioning in another guise, visible already in the accuracy that the standard form's classical solve loses at high resolution. The tools for lowering it come from classical numerical analysis, two of which the descriptor transferred: the banded operators of the ultraspherical method \cite{olver2013} and the carried derivatives of first-order-system least squares \cite{cai1994fosls}.

No speed-up over classical solvers is claimed. An analytic solution needs a resolution growing only as $\log(1/\delta)$ for an accuracy $\delta$, and on one axis a degree linear in $N$ matches the ultraspherical method's classical operation count. Due to the reformulation, a preparation whose cost tracks the classical one in order on a regular problem, and nonlinear flows prepared as unique ground states are now possible. An advantage could appear only on several axes, where a classical solver's cost grows at least as the number of unknowns.

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

\paragraph{A gap on several axes.} Whether a residual can keep its gap there decides whether this route can compete with classical spectral solvers at all. The obvious repair, weighting the datum rows by a power of the tangential mode, has been measured: it raises the gap more than the norm and lowers the cost modestly, but the weighted gap still falls with every qubit. The question is now whether any reweighting, alone or with block rescaling, or rows of the kind first-order-system least squares adds to make its functional coercive, can make the residual bound every carried derivative on several axes. The most direct such rows impose the boundary data on the carried derivative blocks, through the data's tangential derivatives, as well as on the field. The solution satisfies them, so they would leave an exact kernel unchanged, and the gap and norm they give on Laplace's equation (R5a) at three to five qubits per axis would test the idea directly. The other candidate is the full ultraspherical reduction, a banded residual on the field alone that removes the inverse in $\G$ without carrying any derivative. Olver and Townsend's bound on its condition number suggests that on a regular problem it would reach the descriptor's $\tilde\Theta(N)$ degree without a block register \cite{olver2013}, but the bound concerns a square system rather than a least-squares residual, and the reduction's gap on singular problems and on two axes is unknown. Building it and measuring one problem of each class, R2a, Legendre's equation (R3) and the heat equation (R5b), would show which classes it serves.

\paragraph{Deriving the block weight.} Block rescaling sets its weight from the equation's coefficients and the resolution, but its two constants were selected by calibration on the benchmark problems and then held fixed, so its check is in-sample. The quotient of the states concentrated near a zero already fixes how $\zeta$ must grow with $N$, so deriving the weight from the equation, its constants and the crossover between the classes included, should be possible. A regular coefficient that dips towards zero, where the classes meet, is where a derived weight would first be tested against the best on a grid. Block rescaling weights the kernel by one constant per block and so stays cheap, whereas an equilibration per amplitude would need a block encoding of its own at a cost linear in the dimension. Whether a diagonal structured in the Chebyshev index, cheap to encode where the general one is not, can do for the degree what diagonal preconditioning does for the ultraspherical method's banded systems \cite{olver2013}, or whether a degree linear in $N$ is the least that a differentiating residual reaches on one axis, remains open.

\paragraph{What the residual carries.} Any condition that annihilates the solution enters the residual as rows, which is how coupled fields and realified complex-valued equations reached the same filter. A complex residual still gives a Hermitian Gram operator, so native complex preparation needs complex loaders and no new filter, and $z'=-\mathrm{i}\pi z$, which R7 carries realified, is the natural first comparison of the two encodings. An observation $f(x_i)=y_i$ would enter as the row $y_sf(x_i)-y_if(x_s)=0$ against the regular datum $f(x_s)=y_s$, but noisy observations replace the exact kernel with a least-squares fit. Whether that fit keeps a gap a filter can use has not been studied. Adding observations of growing noise to a regular problem such as R2a, and following the ground value and the gap, would show how far a physics-informed Hamiltonian can become a data-informed one. Both directions change the rows and leave the filter and its verification as they are. Wu et al.\ built this route from its circuits outward; the evidence of this thesis is that its cost is decided first by the residual, which is best conditioned before any circuit encodes it.
