Ground-state preparation of a differential equation costs what its residual's conditioning costs, and conditioning is a choice of formulation. Wu et al. considered the cost of the circuits that encode their physics-informed effective Hamiltonian, each polynomial in the number of qubits <cit.>. That count prices one query. The number of queries, i.e. the degree of the filter that prepares the ground state, grows as the square root of the encoding's subnormalisation over the gap, and two costs hide in it. The encoder's cost, the excess of the subnormalisation over the Hamiltonian's norm, is the larger factor in the degree Wu et al.'s circuits imply, and an exact encoding removes it. The Hamiltonian's cost remains. In the standard form, whose state holds the field alone and eliminates every derivative through the dense differentiation matrix , it grows as  for an order- equation with constant coefficients, and no encoding of that residual lowers it under any polynomial filter of the walk. The descriptor form writes the same solution through a residual that carries the intermediate derivatives as unknowns, tied by banded factors, and on a regular problem, one axis with polynomial coefficients, a leading coefficient without zeros on  and analytic data, its degree grows as . The price is paid in the gap, which holds only where the equation controls every derivative the state carries. On nonlinear equations the same view decides what can be prepared at all. Wu et al.'s doubled space has a degenerate ground space and only projects; a Carleman lift has a unique ground state and is prepared, and that uniqueness certifies the lifted linear problem without certifying the lift's convergence to the nonlinear solution. Neither form is polylogarithmic in . The reformulation lowers the power of , and it does so by changing the residual while the circuits stay polynomial.

The consequences reach beyond this method. A polynomial gate count per query is how Wu et al. costed their construction, and it is accurate and incomplete: the degree that multiplies it is set by the encoding's subnormalisation and the Hamiltonian's conditioning, neither of which a gate count registers. Separating the encoder's cost, the excess of the subnormalisation over the norm, from the Hamiltonian's, the norm against the gap, is an audit that any filter-based algorithm on a block encoding admits, and a comparison of formulations needs both halves of it. A comparison as built mixes the two, and favours whichever form has the tighter encoding; only at the ideal-encoding bound  does it compare the Hamiltonians themselves. The degree also has the form of the optimal law for linear systems, with the residual's effective condition number in place of the matrix's, so preparing a ground state meets the conditioning that limits a linear-system solver in another form, as every route to a quantum solver pays a ratio its discretisation fixes. The tools for lowering that ratio are those of classical numerical analysis. The descriptor form transferred two of them, the banded differentiation of the ultraspherical method <cit.> and the carried derivatives of first-order-system least squares <cit.>, and the rest of that body of work, preconditioning and the weighted norms that make least-squares functionals coercive among it, bears as directly on a quantum solver's degree as on a classical solver's condition number.

What becomes possible is narrower than a speed-up and new to this route. On a regular problem the degree grows as , the order of a classical spectral solver's operation count, where the standard form's exceeds it by a factor . Nonlinear equations can be prepared through a Carleman lift run as a coupled linear system, up to two-dimensional incompressible Navier–Stokes in exact emulation, and the condition under which such a lift converges is weaker in practice than in proof. Liu et al. prove that the truncation error falls geometrically when the ratio  of nonlinearity to dissipation is below one, a worst case over all data of a given norm <cit.>; on structured data that condition is far from necessary, and the lift's convergence follows the ratio  of the data's amplitude to the viscosity instead. Past that boundary the lift keeps a unique, isolated ground state, and any method that relies on a truncated lift's spectrum therefore certifies the truncated problem and needs a separate test of the truncation. The residual, finally, acts as an interface. Any condition that annihilates the solution enters as rows, so coupled fields, complex-valued linear equations after realification and, in principle, observed data reach the same filter and the same verification unchanged. The equations that motivate quantum solvers in engineering, flows and fields on several axes, are those this interface reaches most easily and those on which the formulation is least secure.

The first boundary is where the conditioning goes once it leaves the norm. Carrying the derivatives moves the conditioning of differentiation from the Hamiltonian's norm into its gap, because the descriptor measures a state's residual against the derivatives it carries, and its gap stays bounded below only where the equation controls each of them. That control is a discrete coercivity, the property on which first-order-system least squares also rests, and it fails in two ways. Where the leading coefficient vanishes, the equation stops controlling the top derivative near the zero; an a priori rescaling of the blocks repairs this on one axis, without ancilla, at a degree that grows as about . On two axes the equation constrains only a sum of top derivatives, and no rescaling holds the gap. Reading this second failure as lost coercivity is an inference from the mechanism and has not been measured. Its consequence is structural. On one axis a degree linear in  offers no asymptotic advantage over a classical spectral solver, which needs  operations, so an advantage could appear only on several axes, where a classical solver's cost grows as the number of unknowns, and that is where the boundary falls.

The second boundary lies between a prepared state and an answer, and every result here crossed it with the answer in hand. The filter's degree needs the spectral edge, which the flat-gap theorem fixes in order but, being proved by compactness, not in constant, and which every degree here takes from a classical solution of the problem. The lift's convergence was judged against a classical Galerkin solution. The field was decoded classically from amplitudes that a quantum computer exposes only through measurement, and recovering all of it takes of order  estimates, as many as a classical solver on one axis needs operations. In the regime where the method would matter none of these references exists. Nor is width the obstacle to running it: the qubit counts stay modest, and the depth of a filter's queries applied in sequence places even the smallest preparation on error-corrected hardware.

The question these boundaries leave most open is whether any residual keeps its gap on several axes, since the answer decides whether this route can compete with classical spectral solvers at all. First-order-system least squares meets the same loss of coercivity with boundary residuals in fractional-order norms, which suggests weighting the datum rows of Laplace's equation (R5a) by a diagonal that grows with the Chebyshev index. Such weights leave an exact kernel unchanged, so the gap and the norm they produce, measured at three to five qubits per axis, would test the idea directly. The other candidate is the full ultraspherical reduction, a banded residual on the field alone that removes the inverse in  without carrying any derivative; whether it keeps a norm of  without eroding the gap is unknown, and one problem of each class, R2a, Legendre's equation and the heat equation, would settle it. Beneath both lies a question about the floor. Block rescaling changes the kernel by a known diagonal, one weight per block, and so stays cheap, where an equilibration per amplitude would need a diagonal block encoding of its own at a cost linear in the dimension. Whether a diagonal structured in the Chebyshev index, cheap to encode as the general one is not, can do for the degree what diagonal preconditioning does for the ultraspherical method's banded systems <cit.>, or whether a degree linear in  is the least a differentiating residual reaches on one axis, is open.

A second direction would take the classical reference out of the loop, since beyond classical reach a ground-state solver must certify its own output. An underestimated gap raises the degree only as the inverse square root of the bound, so a lower bound of the right order in  would preserve every law measured here. For constant coefficients that bound could come from a quantitative flat-gap theorem that replaces the compactness step with the explicit solution operator of the equation and its constraints, checked against the gaps already measured on the regular problems. Singular and two-axis problems have no theorem to sharpen, and their edge would have to be estimated on the circuit from the eigenphases of the qubitised walk, a harder task because an initial state chosen without the answer has no reason to weigh the edge. The lift admits a test that reads no reference: whether the fields decoded at consecutive Carleman orders separate convergence from divergence can be checked classically on the references already computed, which would also locate the boundary that Burgers' lifts bracket without resolving. The output needs the same treatment, since every cost here stops at the prepared state, and a shot-count study of Wu et al.'s interferometric readout on R2a would supply the factor those costs omit.

The last direction concerns what the residual should carry. A complex residual still gives a Hermitian Gram operator, so native complex preparation needs complex loaders rather than a new filter, and , which R7 carries realified, is the natural first comparison of the two encodings. An observation  would enter as the row  against the regular datum , but noisy observations replace the exact kernel with a least-squares fit, and whether that fit keeps a gap a filter can use has not been studied; it marks where a physics-informed Hamiltonian would become a data-informed one. Each of these changes the rows and leaves the filter and its verification as they are. The work that remains is therefore work on the formulation, which is the shift this thesis argues for: Wu et al. built the route from its circuits outward, and its cost is decided by the residual, which is best conditioned before it is encoded.
